Magnetoelectric systems offer potential for device applications exploiting coupled states between electric and magnetic properties. Among magnetoelectric materials, Fe4Nb2O9 has attracted special attention because of its pronounced dielectric signal at high magnetic transition temperatures. However, the magnetic ground state, which is essential information for understanding its unusual magnetoelectricity, remains unclarified. Here, we report a noncollinear magnetic ground state of Fe4Nb2O9. To examine the magnetoelectric effect associated with sequential magnetic and structural transitions upon cooling, we conducted combined x-ray diffraction, magnetic susceptibility, magnetization, dielectric constant, and magnetodielectric experiments. Powder neutron diffraction experiments revealed a series of magnetic Bragg peaks and clear splitting of peaks via structural transition. Magnetic Rietveld refinements, combined with group theory analysis, determined a noncollinear antiferromagnetic structure including a significant _c_ -axis moment component at 1.5 K. This study provides insights into the understanding of its magnetoelectric properties. 

## **I. INTRODUCTION** 

Magnetoelectric (ME) and multiferroic materials are compounds that exhibit coupling between the electric and magnetic orders [1, 2]. This coupling enables magnetic (electric polarization) control via electric (magnetic) fields. Such coupling holds fundamental significance and promises technological applications, including energy-efficient and next-generation memory devices [3]. 

The study of magnetoelectrics was triggered by an original theoretical conjecture on the ME effect by considering symmetries [4], where spatial-inversion and time-reversal symmetry breaking enabled the coupled state and was subsequently verified experimentally in Cr2O3 [5]. Its magnetic state lacks ferroelectricity under ambient conditions but responds proportionally to an external magnetic field through inversion symmetry breaking—a characteristic termed “linear magnetoelectricity” for this material class. 

In type-II multiferroics, magnetically induced ferroelectricity arises because the magnetic ordering breaks the inversion symmetry, generating electric polarization [6]. Well-known examples include TbMnO3 [7] and TbMn2O5 [8]. However, transition temperatures are typically limited to a few tens of kelvins, hampering practical applications. Therefore, materials with strong ME or unconventional effects are urgently needed for technological applications. 

Honeycomb-based ME antiferromagnets _A_ 4 _B_ 2O9 ( _A_ = Mn, Fe, Co and _B_ = Nb, Ta) [9] have recently attracted

In _A_ 4 _B_ 2O9, the interplay between different valence states of the magnetic ions and two sublattices results in versatile ME properties, such as magnetodielectric effects in Mn4Nb2O9 [10], anisotropic magnetoelectricity in Mn4Ta2O9 [11], manipulation of electric polarization with a rotating magnetic field within the honeycomb plane in Co4Nb2O9 [12], and nonlinear and anisotropic ME effects in Co4Ta2O9 [13]. In this study, Fe4Nb2O9 is examined because of its unusual ME properties. Its magnetic transition temperature is relatively high ( _∼_ 90 K) [16] with an exceptionally large magnetic-order-induced dielectric change [17]. Furthermore, Fe4Nb2O9 exhibits the largest polarization within the family [15]. However, the origins of these effects are unclear because of an ongoing controversy regarding the magnetic order and magnetic space group (MSG). The antiferromagnetic structure of Fe4Nb2O9 was initially proposed to be collinear in the _ab_ plane by powder neutron diffraction [18] in _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ (No. 15.88) and _C_ 2 _[′] /c_ (No. 15.87) MSGs. ## **II. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS** 

Polycrystalline Fe4Nb2O9 samples were prepared by a solid-state reaction, as described elsewhere [16]. Stoichiometric amounts of Fe, Fe2O3, and Nb2O5 powders were thoroughly mixed and then pelletized in a glove box filled with argon. The pellets were then sealed under a vacuum in a quartz tube and heated at 1000 _[◦]_ C for 15 h in a box furnace. The calcined pellet was finely reground and sintered at 1100 _[◦]_ C for 24 h. Powder X-ray diffraction was conducted at room temperature to verify the main phase and minimize the magnetic impurity phase Fe4Nb2O9 [17, 20] using Cu _K α_ radiation. Magnetic susceptibility was measured at 0.05 T between 2 and 300 K, and magnetization data were collected at 2.5 K (from _−_ 9 to 9 T) using a vibrating sample magnetometer in a physical properties measurement system (Quantum Design). The dielectric constant was measured at 100 kHz using an LCR meter for magnetic field (from _−_ 9 to 9 T) and temperature. 

Time-of-flight neutron diffraction measurements were performed on the optimally grown polycrystalline sample on Wide angle in a Single Histogram (WISH) beamline at ISIS, United Kingdom [21]. During the data reduction, time-of-flight data were summed by pairing detector bank data from opposite sides of the scattering angle. The resulting data were categorized into detectors labeled banks 1 to 5 by pairing banks with the same scattering angle on the opposite side of the instrument, corresponding to the ascending order of the specific scattering angles 27.08 _[◦]_ , 58.33 _[◦]_ , 90 _[◦]_ , 121.66 _[◦]_ , and 152.83 _[◦]_ , respectively. Group theory analysis based on the Bilbao crystallographic server identified candidate MSGs [22]. Nuclear and magnetic refinements were conducted using the JANA2006 package [23]. Reciprocal lattice units are presented in the parent trigonal lattice otherwise specified. Peak indexing for the monoclinic or triclinic lattices is also addressed where applicable. 

## **III. COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS** 

First-principles calculations based on density functional theory were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [24]. The exchangecorrelation energy was treated within the framework of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional [25]. The planewave energy cutoff was set to 1100 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled using a 3 _×_ 6 _×_ 2 MonkhorstPack _k_ -point mesh [26]. The interaction between valence and core electrons was described using the projector augmented-wave method [27, 28]. To account for the on-site Coulomb interaction of localized Fe 3 _d_ electrons, the GGA+ _U_ formalism was adopted in the Dudarev approach [29], with the effective Hubbard _U_ parameter ( _U_ eff = _U_ – _J_ ) varied from 3 to 7 eV.

## **IV. RESULTS** 

## **A. Coupled magnetic and electric properties** 

This study utilized high-quality polycrystalline Fe4Nb2O9 powder with a minimized impurity phase, as confirmed by x-ray diffraction. Figure 2(a) presents Rietveld refinement results on x-ray diffraction patterns obtained at room temperature. The trigonal structure was confirmed ( _P_[¯] 3 _c_ 1, No. 165) to be consistent with the literature [16–18]. The Bragg peaks were indexed with the target phase Fe4Nb2O9 with tiny impurity peaks from FeNb2O6, and Nb2O5 (a level of 1% mass volume). 

We then performed a combined measurement of magnetic susceptibility, magnetization, and dielectric constant measurements to examine the magnetoelectric properties of Fe4Nb2O9. Figure 2(b) displays the zerofield-cooled (ZFC) and field-cooled (FC) susceptibilities _χ_ at 0.05 T. A ZFC-FC bifurcation revealed susceptibility anomaly, indicating a long-range antiferromagnetic transition at _T_ N _∼_ 90 K. This is consistent with neutron diffraction results (which will be discussed later). High-temperature fitting was performed on the ZFC data between 120 and 300 K by using the Curie-Weiss (CW) equation _χ_ ( _T_ ) = _χ_ 0 + _C/_ ( _T − θ_ ), where _χ_ 0 is temperature-independent susceptibility, _C_ is the Curie constant, and _θ_ is the CW temperature [30]. It displays a linear feature, suggesting a consistent dominant antiferromagnetic interaction. A spin-flop transition was also observed at around 2.45 T, which was consistently reported in the literature [16, 31, 32].

## **B. Successive magnetic and structural transitions upon cooling** 

Figure 3 presents the neutron diffraction patterns obtained from 120 to 1.5 K, focusing on the main magnetic and nuclear Bragg peaks. The top and bottom rows of 

Fig. 3 display the contour plot and stacked data, respectively. They reveal two phase transitions at 97.5 K ( _T_ N) and 87.5 K ( _T_ S), consistent with observed anomalies in the magnetic and electric measurements (Fig. 2). At 97.5 K, the emergence of additional signals for specific Bragg peaks indicates the onset of long-range antiferromagnetic order at (004), (100), and (002) with values of 7.09, 4.52, and 3.55[˚] A, respectively [Figs. 3(c), 3(g) and 3(i), with the corresponding temperature-dependent data given in Figs. 3(d), 3(h) and 3(j)]. In contrast, the intensities of other peaks, such as (104), (1–14), (102), and (1–12), exhibit no significant changes [Figs. 3(a), 3(b), 3(e), and 3(f)]. 

Below 87.5 K, several Bragg peaks strongly split, except for (00 _l_ ) peaks. The previously degenerate Bragg peaks, (104) (1–14), (102), and (1-12), began to split as shown in Figs. 3(a), 3(b), 3(e), and 3(f). This indicates a structural distortion in the _ab_ plane, consistent with the previously reported monoclinic structure [17, 18]. Upon further cooling, magnetic Bragg peak intensities gradually increase, while nuclear peaks exhibit progressively stronger splitting. Between 87.5 and 70 K, the trigonal and monoclinic structures coexist (see the green dashed lines in the top row and the shaded areas in the bottom row of Fig. 3), with decreasing trigonal peak intensity and increasing monoclinic peak intensity.

## **C. Magnetic and structural refinements** 

Magnetic and structural refinements were then conducted for quantitative analysis using neutron diffraction data. The nuclear structure at 120 K (well above _T_ N) was first determined to serve as a reference for magnetic refinements at lower temperatures, consistent with the known crystal structure [17, 18]. Because additional peaks, which were observed below _T_ N, are allowed in the parent space group, we interpreted them as magnetic Bragg peaks indexed by the ordering wave vector _k_ = (0, 0, 0). Group theory analysis using _k_ and the parent crystal structure provided candidates for MSGs (see the details in the Appendix and Fig. 4). 

Our observations of the peak splitting align with two previous studies [17, 18], in which the structural transition from the trigonal ( _P_[¯] 3 _c_ 1) to the monoclinic space group ( _C_ 2 _/c_ ) was proposed to be below _T_ S. This trend remained unchanged from 70 to 1.5 K. Thus, refinements of the magnetic and nuclear structures were refined at 1.5 K—below the base temperatures of 10 K [18] and 5 K [17] reported in the literature—to determine a more accurate magnetic ground state using high-resolution neutron diffraction data with better statistics.

## **D.** _Ab initio_ **calculations** 

As a result, we present the refinement results for four different magnetic models in Figs. 5. They compare the refinement outcomes at 1.5 K using a selective set of magnetic models. These results demonstrate that our highresolution neutron diffraction data can distinguish and rule out two previously reported magnetic models without the _Mc_ component [Figs. 5(a) and 5(b)], providing two better solutions with the _Mc_ moment [Figs. 5(c) and 5(d)]. We note that the different models exhibit similar magnetic Bragg peaks. However, the predicted intensities of the Bragg peaks vary significantly, depending on the specific models, the spin moments, and the number of distinct magnetic sublattices. Figure 7 allows us to visually inspect these differences. 

Figure 7 compares the reported magnetic structures with ours. A previous powder neutron diffraction study [18] found a collinear antiferromagnetic order at 10 K, as visualized in Fig. 7(a). The spins along the _c_ axis are aligned parallel and antiparallel within the _ab_ plane, forming a simple antiferromagnetic N´eel phase in the honeycomb lattice stacked along the _c_ axis. The other previous study using single-crystal neutron diffraction data reported the same magnetic structure with the same _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ MSG, except for the presence of a slight canting angle of 5.81 _[◦]_ between the pairs of ferromagnetic chains [17] [Fig. 7(b)]. However, we discovered a substantially distinct magnetic order even in the same _C_ 2 _[′] /c_ MSG [Fig. 5(c)]. The magnetic moments in the two sublattices were unequal, which may be more realistic given their chemically distinct positions, different Wyckoff sites, and varying oxygen environments. The magnetic moments of the flat layer (Fe1) were consistently smaller than those of the buckled layer (Fe2) across models. The refined magnetic structure with _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ is shown in Fig. 7(d) for comparison. 

We further performed _ab initio_ calculations to compare the energy of the six magnetic models (A to F; see Table III). All crystal structures were fixed to that of model F to provide a consistent basis for comparing the energies of the models. The unit cell size differs only slightly among the six models. The magnetic configurations were introduced based on the experimental results (Table III) by initializing the Fe magnetic moments. During the self-consistent field iterations, the magnetic moment directions initialized for each configuration remained stable, indicating that the energy landscape respects the imposed magnetic symmetries. 

Figure 8 shows the total energy differences using the energy of model F subtracted as the reference. Model F is found to be the energetically most favorable configuration within the range of _U_ eff = 4.5 to 6.0 eV, which is a sensible range for the Fe[2+] correlation energy, with similar energies in models C, E, and F. This theoretical result is consistent with a small difference in _R_ factor values from experiments, as shown in Fig. 5 and Table I. The relatively lower total energies of models C to F in comparison to those of models A and B suggest that magnetic configurations featuring the finite out-of-plane spin component are energetically preferred in the calculation, too. 

## **V. DISCUSSION** 

In this work, we determined a new magnetic structure of Fe4Nb2O9 at 1.5 K using the _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ MSG and then extended the analysis down to the highest possible ( _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ ) MSG compatible with all nine ME components. ## **VI. CONCLUSIONS**

A previous study observed an anisotropic ME effect and electric polarization reversal, explained by a metalligand _p_ - _d_ hybridization mechanism in the _P_[¯] 3 _c_ 1 and _C_ 2 _/c_ space group [15]. However, this mechanism fails to explain the net electric polarization under _H ∥ c_ , as the in-plane magnetic moments were still antiparallel in the two previous magnetic structural candidates [17, 18]. This issue can be resolved using our magnetic models where the in-plane spins do not cancel under _H ∥ c_ (models C and F). Also, the ME tensor of the _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ MSG at both ambient and high-field states [19] cannot explain the observed electric polarization for all nine components [34]. On the other hand, for the magnetic structure using _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ , all nine components of the ME tensor are possible by symmetry even at ambient magnetic fields. This might be consistent with the electric polarization experimentally observed under the strong magnetic field for all nine directions [15]. The ME tensors of the _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ and _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ MSGs are compared in Table II. If _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ is chosen as the magnetic ground state of Fe4Nb2O9, the additional ME tensor components of _α_ 12, _α_ 21, _α_ 23, and _α_ 32 cannot be explained. However, revisited ME measurements on single crystals without or with weak magnetic fields will be needed to confirm the ambient magnetic symmetry. The ME analysis based on the magnetic structure established in this study could advance the understanding of its unconventional ME properties in future studies. 

The structure using _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ has been presented through a comprehensive experimental and theoretical analysis in this study. However, we emphasize that the qualitative differences in the reliability factors compared to those of _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ are minimal in terms of refinement results. Additionally, the difference in the calculated ground state energies between these two magnetic space groups is negligible (Fig. 8). To further verify the magnetic ground state and distinguish between these two MSGs, it will be crucial to observe the peak splitting in the triclinic structure experimentally. In this regard, high-resolution x-ray diffraction could be useful in future studies. 

Compared to Fe4Nb2O9, the related compound 

This study established a more realistic magnetic structure for honeycomb magnetoelectric Fe4Nb2O9 through powder neutron diffraction. High-quality data combined with group analysis enabled us to determine the out-ofplane magnetic moment perpendicular to the honeycomb layer. Previously reported magnetic structures within the same _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ MSG without the _Mc_ component were not fitted with our data. Thus, they can be ruled out as candidates for the magnetic ground state. Furthermore, motivated by the experimental observation of the complete ME tensor components, we provided the magnetic structure in a lower MSG, _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ , where the _Mc_ is also substantial. This study provides key information—the MSG and its symmetry—crucial for understanding unusually strong ME properties of honeycomb noncollinear antiferromagnetic Fe4Nb2O9.

## **Appendix A1: Group theory analysis** 

In this Appendix, we provide the details of the group theory analysis. A group-subgroup symmetry analysis was conducted utilizing the K-SUBGROUPSMAG program from the Bilbao Crystallographic Server [22], in conjunction with JANA2006 [23]. Due to a structural phase transition that occurs upon cooling, characterized by peak splitting, the parent trigonal structure transitions to a monoclinic lattice. Consequently, the analysis was performed using the low-temperature monoclinic structure, which was previously reported at 40 K [17]. A group-subgroup diagram was obtained using the crystal structure and the propagation wave vector _k_ = (0, 0, 0) (Fig. 4). Among the _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ (No. 15.88) and _C_ 2 _[′] /c_ (No. 15.87) MSGs, we found that the former presented a good fit with the data collected at 1.5 K. 

To further investigate the potential for lower symme- 

tries, we also conducted magnetic and structural refinements using subgroups of _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ , such as _Cc[′]_ , _C_ 2, and _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ , as illustrated in Fig. 5. The refinement results are presented in Table III, where the Fe ions are divided into two independent sites for the subgroups of _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ . Further, Table IV explicitly shows the magnetic symmetry relations for two candidate MSGs, _C_ 2 _/c[′]_ and _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ , obtained from the monoclinic lattice. Given the symmetries, a finite _Mc_ is allowed for both subgroups. Note that the same subgroup tree is derived when utilizing the parent trigonal structure; however, the number of atoms is halved since it started from the trigonal lattice. 

The _P_ 1 MSG, a subgroup of _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ , is also feasible based on our group analysis, as shown in Fig. 4. It also accommodates all nine magnetoelectric components. However, in this study, we selected _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ as the magnetic ground state due to its higher symmetry. This approach is standard for determining the magnetic space group, allowing a simpler explanation of the material with a larger number of available symmetry operators. The _P_[¯] 1 _[′]_ MSG is the highest symmetric candidate accommodating all nine ME components, based on our group theory analysis, as shown in Fig. 4.
