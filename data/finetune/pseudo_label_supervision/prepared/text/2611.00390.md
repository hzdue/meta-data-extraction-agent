Chiral magnetic materials can host skyrmions and hence are of interest for numerous spintronic applications including low-power storage. To reach this goal, room temperature operation and a thin film form are needed. We have studied the chiral magnetism in a _β_ –Mn structured CoZn sputtered film, with a Curie temperature of _≈_ 130 °C, by means of polarized neutron reflectometry (PNR) in order to reveal its magnetic field-temperature phase diagram. In bulk this material has a helimagnetic ground state. Here we use PNR data to show that the helimagnetism survives in a CoZn film only 100 nm thick, roughly half a helical period. Given the subtlety of differentiating the helical state from a uniform state with reduced interfacial magnetism, we have developed and employed a Bayesian approach that uses model selection to analyze our PNR data and to map out the field-temperature phase diagram of our thin film. 

## **I. INTRODUCTION** 

he archetypal materials for the study of skyrmions are the magnetic materials with the B20 crystal T structure [1]. This class includes 3d transition metal silicides and germanides, the foundational materials being the monosilicide MnSi [2] and monogermanide FeGe [3, 4]. Interest in these materials was spurred by the resolution of their unusual magnetic properties being due to a long range magnetic state identified as a helical magnetic state (‘helical spin density wave’) [5, 6]. Notably, small angle neutron scattering (SANS) was used by Ishikawa _et al._ [6] for MnSi to directly measure the helix wavelength. Later, the helical wavelength in FeGe would be measured, again using SANS [7]. 

From here new ternary silicide compounds were explored, chiefly FeCoSi and CoMnSi [8] which also showed long range helical magnetic order. It followed that topological ‘vortex’ states were predicted in condensed matter systems [9, 10], and in 2006 magnetic materials with chiral interactions were predicted to be capable of hosting skyrmions [11]. This was soon followed by the breaking of new ground with the publication by M¨uhlbauer _et al._ [12], which reported on their findings of skyrmions in a magnetic system, namely in the MnSi system measured once again using SANS. The confirmation of the predicted skyrmions in a magnetic metal lead to a surge in related research across the B20 class and beyond. Within bulk systems SANS is used extensively to directly measure magnetic helices [13–16].

None of the materials described above exhibit robust magnetic order at or above room temperature. It has long been known that CoZnMn alloys, which are room temperature ferromagnets, possess a _β_ -Mn phase [17– 20]. This is a chiral structure with space group _P_ 4332 or _P_ 4132, depending on the handedness. Around a decade ago, CoZnMn was shown, in the bulk, to host skyrmions at room temperature [21] and a flurry of other studies since that time have explored their properties [22–27]. These metastable skyrmion phases [28], as in the B20 materials, are based on a helimagnetic ground state. 

The origin of these helical magnetic structures is explained by the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) which arises from the chirality of the crystal structure [29, 30]. The interplay of Heisenberg exchange, which alone typically gives rise to collinear states, with the DMI gives rise to a chiral canting between neighboring spins and hence a long-range spin helix. Identification of helical order is therefore a significant experimental hurdle, since the most that can be expected is subtle deviations from a state that is otherwise uniformly magnetized along the field direction. ## **II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS** 

## **A. Sample Growth/Preparation**

Within a thin-film it becomes necessary to refine this understanding of the helical spin structure. In the bulk, one would expect the helical axis to reorient itself relative to an applied field, however in a thin film we are constrained by the shape anisotropy. In this instance we adopt the term “helicoid” to describe the magnetic structure in a helimagnetic film as it extends through the plane(s) of the film [36–38]. The helicoid describes the resulting structure of a helimagnet under an applied field, _H_ , perpendicular to the helical axis, _Q_ , while constrained by anisotropy, _K_ . This causes the helix to distort, with moments canting towards the the applied field direction, instead of reorientating the axis, which effectively unwinds the helix thus changing the period. This behavior, where the helix wavevector Q is fixed normal to the film plane and the helix unwinds with applied inplane field, has been seen to occur in thin-films of both MnSi [36, 39] and FeGe [37]. 

Thin film samples of amorphous/polycrystalline cobalt-zinc were deposited by co-sputtering from elemental targets. These are grown on a 1000[˚] A SiO2 surface layer of a 0.5 mm thick thermally oxidized Si substrate. This is done in balanced magnetrons with an argon working gas at a growth pressure of 3.2 mTorr. The deposition system has a base pressure of approximately 10 _[−]_[8] Torr. The deposition power of the Co and Zn guns were controlled separately to set the stoichiometry of the deposited alloy film. 

To promote the growth of the _β_ -Mn crystal structure, we annealed the sample post-deposition. The sample was isolated in a quartz glass ampule, repeatedly pumppurged with 99.9999 % pure argon gas before being evacuated to a roughing pressure of order 10 _[−]_[1] _∼_ 10 _[−]_[2] Torr. 

The annealing profile staggered decreasing ramp rates of 5/3/1 °C per minute relative to temperatures of 300/350/400 °C, with relative dwell times of 2/1/72 hours; before finally being cooled at the natural rate of the furnace with the output turned off. Full details of sample preparation methods are described in Refs 33 and 34.

## **B. X-Ray Reflectometry/Diffraction** 

X-ray reflectometry (XRR) and diffraction (XRD) were performed using a Rigaku Smartlab rotating anode diffractometer. The diffractometer was set up in a Bragg-Brentano geometry with a Cu anode source and Ge (220) 2-bounce monochromator, and was run in continuous scanning mode at 0.5 deg/min with a resolution of 0.0002 deg for both XRR and XRD. 

## **C. Magnetometry** 

Magnetometry was performed using a superconducting quantum interference device vibrating sample magnetometer (SQUID-VSM), equipped with an oven module option which operates the sample chamber at a pressure of _<_ 100 mTorr. This option allows for temperatures _T_ up to 700 °C to be safely achieved by way of direct contact of the heating element with the sample. 

We measured a series of magnetization ( _M_ ) hysteresis loops ( _M_ ( _H_ )) from a _µ_ 0 _H_ = +700 mT saturating field to _−_ 700 mT and back again. The hysteresis loops were obtained at the temperatures of 26.85, 105.85, 126.85, and 155.85 °C, where the final loop was acquired well above the Curie temperature of CoZn of _T_ C _≈_ 129 °C. These were close to the PNR measurement temperatures of 25, 106, 127, and 156 °C. 

## **D. Polarized Neutron Reflectivity** 

The depth dependent magnetization profiles were measured using PNR on the POLREF beamline at the ISIS Neutron and Muon Source. Sample heating was conducted using a bespoke vacuum furnace (base pressure of _∼_ 10 _[−]_[7] mbar), which was itself mounted within an electromagnet using a 100 mm pole gap. The sample was mounted onto a copper mounting block with a thin layer of thermal conducting grease before being loaded into the furnace and pumped down to _<_ 10 _[−]_[5] mbar prior to heating and measurement. 

The sample was set into its initial magnetic state by heating to 156 °C (above the Curie temperature, _T_ C _≈_ 129 °C) under a 700 mT applied field, then field cooled to the required temperature in an applied field of _µ_ 0 _H_ = 0 _._ 9 mT. At this point PNR data were taken out to a maximum wavevector transfer of _Qz_ = 0 _._ 12[˚] A _[−]_[1] . Primary isotherms scans were taken at 25 °C and 106 °C, and a primary isofield scan at _µ_ 0 _H_ = 0 _._ 9 mT. For each isotherm, the field was increased incrementally up to a maximum value of _µ_ 0 _H_ = 700 mT, a value known from hysteresis loop measurements to be sufficient to magnetically saturate the sample [34]. The system was reset as required by heating above the Curie temperature and field cooling to the next required value in an appropriately low guide field to not depolarize the neutron beam. This allowed monitoring with PNR to determine when 

the system had reached thermal equilibrium and a stable neutronic sample alignment was obtainable. 

## **III. RESULTS** 

## **A. X-Ray Reflectometry/Diffraction** 

We studied a CoZn film deposited using sputter powers of 36 W for Co and 25 W Zn, close to the optimal ratio to obtain the _β_ -Mn structure without excess Co particulates [34], with nominal thickness of 1000[˚] A. As the helical period is reported to be 1850–2100 A for Co[˚] 10Zn10 _β_ -Mn in bulk [21], this thickness then represents approximately half of the bulk period. 

The XRD pattern of the post-annealed film, presented in Fig. 2(a), confirms the expected predominant _⟨_ 221 _⟩ β_ - Mn texture alongside the presence of the ZnO capping layer [34]. 

Fig. 2(b) displays the corresponding XRR measurement, featuring clear and regular Kiessig fringes indicative of high structural smoothness. The overall fringe intensity envelope is consistent with the presence of a capping layer. Fitting of the XRR data was performed using the GenX code [40], utilizing a model in which the active CoZn layer is split into three sub-layers: an interfacial layer adjacent to the substrate, a bulk layer, and a top layer interfacing with the ZnO cap. 

The resulting fit yields a total CoZn film thickness of _∼_ 976[˚] A and an interfacial roughness of _∼_ 44[˚] A. The analysis indicates a minimal surface ZnO thickness ( _∼_ 66[˚] A), which is comparable to the surface roughness of the underlying CoZn film ( _∼_ 44[˚] A). The scattering length density (SLD) depth profile extracted from the fit is shown in Fig. 2(c). This reveals a slight SLD depletion in the CoZn film at the interface with the SiO2 substrate ( _∼_ 0 _._ 55% reduction extending across _∼_ 72[˚] A), but with the vast majority of the film consisting of the bulk behavior. 

Overall, these structural results confirm that the film possesses the intended morphology of a _β_ -Mn ordered layer of CoZn with a ZnO capping layer [34], with a high degree of interfacial smoothness that renders it suitable for subsequent PNR analysis.

Due to its magnetic depth sensitivity, PNR is an ideal probe to investigate the depth dependent inplane magnetic texture present within the sample. Here, PNR was used to determine if the sample was in one of two magnetic states - helical or uniform, as illustrated in Fig. 1 - at various points in phase space. We measured

The same comparison as shown in Fig. 4 was conducted for all data taken within this study: both helical and uniform models were fitted to the data for each value of field and temperature at which the sample was measured. A common method of performing model selection is to compare the goodness-of-fit (in our case reduced _χ_[2] ) of the best fit parameters. However, there are issues with purely using a goodness-of-fit metric as a model selection criterion. For example, this methodology does not take into account variation in the model parameters allowed by variance in the data (error in reflectivity), which can in principle produce similar goodness-of-fit for quite different parameter sets. This is a problem that is particularly acute in reflectometry as a result of the inverse phase problem [43]: the loss of the phase information allows for multiple solutions for the same reflectivity profile, i.e. a loss of uniqueness. Here we use the Bayesian model selection approach of calculating and comparing the Bayesian model evidence [44–47] to indicate whether the helical or uniform magnetization profiles are more probable for each of the field-temperature conditions measured.

## _1. Model selection_ 

Bayesian model selection relies on comparing the probability of one model to another with respect to the data. In order to do this, we need to calculate the probability of each model to be compared ( _Mi_ ) given the data ( _d_ ) is true - otherwise known as the posterior probability _P_ ( _Mi|d_ ) [48]. To compare 2 models, the ratio of the posterior probabilities - the posterior odds ratio - is calculated. For models _M_ 1 and _M_ 2, the posterior odds ratio is defined as [44, 47]:

The value obtained from calculating the posterior odds ratio for two models _M_ 1 and _M_ 2, provides a metric whereby a strength of model preference can be quantified - numbers greater than 1 favor the model on the numerator, while numbers less than 1 indicate the inverse. In the case of this study, we have no prior preference for the helical or uniform magnetization models for any given measurement condition, so the prior odds are assumed to be 1. The posterior odds ratio is then just simply the Bayes factor _B_ . Bayes factors were calculated for all measurement conditions taken in this study and used to determine a model preference of helical or uniform in each case.

In particular the highest measurement temperature of 156 °C, the film is above _T_ C and so the presence of helimagnetism is impossible, consistent with our findings.

## _3. Helicoid profile_ 

The ratio between the two amplitudes _A_ 1 and _A_ 2 in Eq. 4 provides information about whether the magnetization depth profile is a purely helical one (magnetic moment is sinusoidal with respect to depth), or a helicoid: the former is observed if _A_ 1 is large and _A_ 2 is negligible, the latter becomes a better description of the profile as _A_ 2 increases. Fig. 8 shows the best fit values for the 25 °C isotherm for applied field values in the range of 5-47 mT. Observing the data, there appears to be a slight reduction in the ratio _A_ 1 _/A_ 2 with increasing applied field. An increase of _A_ 2 alters the profile such that for each rotation of the helix, the moment points more in the direction of the applied field as the strength of the field is increased. This would be consistent with the picture that at greater applied fields the helix transitions to a helicoid, which at high enough applied fields unwinds to a uniform magnetization profile. 

As discussed in §III D 2, the other field-temperature points where the helix was determined to be the more probable model displayed large uncertainties for _A_ 1 and _A_ 2 such that no meaningful trend could be observed for those isotherms.

## **IV. CONCLUSION** 

To conclude, our main result is that we have observed helical ordering in this CoZn thin film by means of PNR coupled with a novel Bayesian approach to data analysis. The transition between ferromagnetic and the helical state is consistent with the bulk material, although the _T_ C appears to be lower (130 °C, consistent with Ref. 34) than in the bulk (200 °C) [21]. The helix pitch _λ_ h is estimated to be 1724[+68] _−_ 54[˚A][(this][is][the][average][of][the][he-] lix pitch values obtained at fields including and between 5-47 mT, where the error is defined by the average the upper and lower bounds of the 95% credible intervals), and is invariant with respect to temperature and field. 

Since the film thickness is comparable to half of the helix pitch, the departure of the _M_ ( _z_ ) profile from that of a uniformly magnetized film is somewhat subtle. We have demonstrated the utility of Bayesian model selection in polarized neutron reflectometry by implementing this method to show that the helical state is overwhelmingly the most probable one for our film for fields below the saturation field at all temperatures below _T_ C, allowing us to reject the model of uniform magnetization under these conditions. Comparison of the _A_ 1 _/A_ 2 ratio indicates potential trend of increasing component of distorted helix with increasing field, which is consistent with observations in FeGe reported in Ref. 37. This makes sense when thinking about the applied field pulling moments into the field direction - slowly shifting from a helix to a helicoid - which eventually becomes a uniform magnetization state. 

Now that we have established that sputtered thin films of _β_ -phase CoZn are capable of showing helical ordering, we hope that our work will promote real-space imaging of chiral spin textures such as skyrmions–e.g. by Lorentz microscopy–as a stepping stone to room temperature pla- 

10 

nar devices based on spin textures such as skyrmions in this fascinating material.

## **1. Structural Nuclear SLD fit** 

The structural nuclear scattering length density (nSLD) of the CoZn sample used in this study is shown in Fig. 9. The structural fit was obtained prior to the specific mSLD model selection analysis described in sections III C and III D. This was to ensure that the only variable we were comparing between models was the magnetic profile. 

Since the structure was assumed to be invariant with respect to temperature, multiple data sets could be used 

in a large co-fit, with the aim to reduce the ambiguity in determining the nSLD. The structural parameters were shared between all datasets which were included in the co-fit, while the magnetic profile was unique for each dataset. The 106 °C isotherm in addition to the 25 °C 700 mT saturated data set, were used to determine the nSLD. While in principle more datasets from the study could have been used in this co-fit, practically the small improvement in uncertainty was outweighed by the large increase in convergence time when trying to include more data - indeed, the full co-fit never converged after significant numbers of iterations due to the very slow convergence rate. 

As this co-fit was conducted before the final magnetic fitting, it was important to model the individual magnetic profiles for each of the datasets with enough freedom so that the structural fit was not skewed by any bias of what the magnetic profile should be. Thus, a freeform spline was chosen to model each magnetic profile. The spline comprised 3-control points which were spaced equidistant in _z_ across the CoZn layer - only the magnitude of each point in mSLD were fitted. This provided enough freedom to the model such that the spline could mimic a helical or uniform profile – effectively removing the bias of the magnetism on the structural fit – while minimizing model complexity such that the fit would still be able to converge. 

Several models of varying complexity were tried to fit the nSLD, but due to the inter-diffuse nature of the CoZn/ZnO interface, the most consistent model to the data comprised spline interfaces between the CoZn and ZnO layers, and at the ZnO/air interface. These interfaces could not be satisfactorily modeled with the standard symmetric error function interface profiles. The rest of the profile was modeled with a traditional layered box model - regions of constant SLD interfaced with symmetric error function profiles. 

When taking into account the different scattering contrasts between X-rays (see Fig.2 panel (c)) and Neutrons, the structural SLD profiles broadly agree. The CoZn thickness is consistent between the two probes (where the CoZn layer and top interface combined total thickness for Neutrons is _∼_ 1054[˚] A and X-rays is _∼_ 1020[˚] A) and the overall interface widths of the top and bottom interfaces of the CoZn layer also follow a similar profile. The SiO2-CoZn interface is sharp in both cases, while the CoZn-ZnO-Air region in both profiles is broad. Due to the improved contrast between CoZn and ZnO, the Neutrons provide a more nuanced picture of the surface interface that X-rays struggle to resolve.
