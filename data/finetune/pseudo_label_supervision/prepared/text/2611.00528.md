Three-dimensional spin textures are stabilized by microscopic interactions within their host materials. X-ray vector tomography can image these nanoscale structures, but weak magnetic contrast and a limited range of viewing angles often leave parts of the magnetization poorly determined. Here we show that a reconstruction constrained by the micromagnetic energy recovers the threedimensional magnetization from such data and gains sensitivity to the underlying material interactions. Applied to the chiral magnet Co8Zn9Mn3, the method resolves the three-dimensional helical texture even from a small fraction of the dual-axis projections typically required for a successful tomographic reconstruction, or from a single tilt axis alone. By testing which ratio of exchange to Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction in the reconstruction best reproduces the measured projections, we obtain a value for the sample that differs by about 10 % from the literature value derived from the helical winding period. Taken together, our findings suggest a way to reduce the time needed for vector tomography and to extend it to determining fundamental magnetic interactions in a material. 

Magnetic materials support a rich family of threedimensional textures, including skyrmion strings [1, 2], hopfions [3], and vortex rings [4]. These textures carry a nontrivial topology that governs how they form, move, and interact, which makes them candidates for threedimensional spintronic devices [5, 6]. To understand the three-dimensional structure and behavior of these textures, we must image the full magnetization vector field. X-ray magnetic vector tomography provides this capability by reconstructing the vector field from projections recorded with X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) contrast [7] about two rotation axes [8– 10]. X-ray vector imaging has since been extended to laminography [11], and electron magnetic tomography has been used to reconstruct magnetic vector fields from holographic [12] or Lorentz [13] images. However, textures that vary across the depth as well as in the plane require samples several hundred nanometers or even micrometers thick. To study these systems, high-resolution imaging has been made possible with the use of hard X- rays [8] and pre-edge phase contrast with coherent soft X- ray techniques [14]. However, weak magnetic scattering can result in noisy dichroic projections. This, combined with practical constraints on accessible measurement angles, radiation dose, and available synchrotron beamtime, 

can result in nanoscale features in the magnetization vector field being poorly constrained [15]. 

To prevent noise from distorting the reconstruction, we can spatially smooth it by penalizing sharp changes in the magnetization [16, 17]. Mathematically, this gradient penalty has the same form as the exchange interaction (stiffness _A_ ) that aligns neighboring moments in micromagnetic theory [18, 19], giving the smoothing a direct physical basis. However, because the experimental resolution often approaches the scale of the magnetic features, this smoothing alone blurs the domain walls and helical winding we may want to image. In micromagnetic theory, stable states are local minima of the full energy functional, of which exchange is only one term. In a real magnet, these features persist because exchange competes with the demagnetizing interaction (scaled by the saturation magnetization _M_ s), which favors flux closure, and in chiral magnets also with the Dzyaloshinskii– Moriya interaction (constant _D_ ), which twists neighboring moments. Previous studies have applied the micromagnetic model only after the reconstruction, adjusting simulations by hand to reproduce the imaged texture [2, 12, 20]. This manual tuning is time-consuming and cannot restore features that the reconstruction has already lost or distorted.

We extend X-ray magnetic vector tomography with the complete micromagnetic energy functional of the sample and demonstrate its two roles on a thick disk of the room-temperature chiral magnet Co8Zn9Mn3 [22], imaged with dichroic soft X-ray ptychography. First, the energy encodes our prior knowledge of the interactions in the material and helps us recover the magnetization vector field where a limited number of noisy projections alone is not enough. Second, because the reconstruction explicitly incorporates these competing interactions, it allows us to test the assumed model itself. Since material parameters are usually only determined for the bulk and do not always translate to a nanostructure, we identify the relevant parameters of the sample that best reproduce the measured projections. This prospect of texture-driven magnetometry on individual nanostructures is relevant for a wide variety of magnetic material systems. 

In magnetic tomography, XMCD imaging records two projections at every sample orientation, with opposite circular X-ray polarization _σ[±]_ , giving a transmission image pair _C_[+] and _C[−]_ [8, 23]. At the X-ray absorption edges of magnetic materials, the XMCD effect provides contrast that is sensitive to the local magnetization component along the beam propagation direction [7] and reverses sign between _σ_[+] and _σ[−]_ . Unlike the scalar density imaged in conventional tomography, the magnetization is a vector field probed only along the beam, so rotating the sample changes not only the viewing angle but also which component is sensed. To reconstruct all three vector components, the acquisition therefore needs to combine two orthogonal tilt series (Fig. 1a) or to use a laminography geometry [11]. Tilting about _y_ ˆ confines the beam to the _xz_ plane, so this series (the _xz_ series) is sensitive to _mx_ and _mz_ but never to ˆ _my_ . Tilting about _x_ confines the beam to the _yz_ plane, so the _yz_ series is sensitive to _my_ and _mz_ but never to _mx_ . 

Recovering the three-dimensional magnetization configuration requires finding a vector field whose forward projections reproduce the measured data (Fig. 1c). Even a complete dual-axis acquisition does not uniquely determine the magnetization: line integrals of **m** _·_ **k**[ˆ] determine at most the divergence-free part of the magnetization [25, 26], since any internal gradient field that vanishes at the sample boundary integrates to zero along every beam path [9]. In physical terms, the magnetic volume and surface charges of the sample cannot be fully recovered without additional constraints. In practice, depending on the sample geometry and the setup, projections cannot always be recorded over the complete angular range, leaving a so-called missing wedge of unrecorded viewing directions in tomography and a missing cone in laminography [27]. Moreover, weak XMCD signals make the projections particularly susceptible to noise and imaging artifacts across the angular range.

## **II. RECONSTRUCTING THE MAGNETIC STATE** 

To demonstrate our physics-informed reconstruction, we apply it to tomographic data measured on a 1 _._ 1 µm-diameter, 300 nm-thick Co8Zn9Mn3 disk to recover its three-dimensional magnetization configuration. Co8Zn9Mn3 belongs to the Co–Zn–Mn family of roomtemperature helimagnets [22], which host skyrmions [22], Bloch points [20], and hopfions [29]. We image the disk in the remanent state, where it hosts a helical configuration. The XMCD phase projections were measured 3 eV below the Co _L_ 3 absorption edge (778 _._ 1 eV) using dichroic soft X-ray ptychography, a coherent diffractive imaging method that recovers the phase shift of the transmitted X-rays. At this pre-edge energy, the X-rays are only weakly attenuated while the magnetic phase contrast persists, which allows imaging of samples up to an order of magnitude thicker than at the absorption edge [14]. The two tilt series cover _θxz ∈_ [ _−_ 41 _[◦] ,_ +15 _[◦]_ ] (29 projections, _xz_ series) and _θyz ∈_ [ _−_ 30 _[◦] ,_ +47 _[◦]_ ] (38 projections, _yz_ series) with a pixel size of about 10 nm. To reconstruct the magnetization with Eq. (2), we choose the energy weight _λ[E]_ and specify the material parameters. We use the literature values of _A_ and _M_ s [20] and _D_ = 0 _._ 66 _×_ 10 _[−]_[3] J m _[−]_[2] , the DMI constant that best reproduces the measured projections (Sec. IV, Extended Data Table I).

## **III. RECONSTRUCTION FROM SPARSE AND SINGLE-AXIS DATA** 

Time-resolved and field-dependent studies require a complete dual-axis tilt series for every time step or field value. Tomograms at successive time steps have been recorded [11], but faster acquisitions would allow for better temporal resolution and a wider range of phenomena and experimental parameters to be explored. To maximize the efficiency of these measurements, we test whether the physics prior can recover the magnetization from reduced projection data, either by sparse angular sampling or by omitting an entire tilt series. We recon- 

struct the disk under four acquisition conditions (Fig. 3): the full dual-axis measurement, a sparse dataset with only every eighth projection of each tilt series (9 projections), and either tilt series alone ( _xz_ series or _yz_ series). All four use _λ[E]_ = 10[15] , ten times the balanced weight _λ[E]_ bal[,][to][compensate][for][the][reduced][number][of] projections. Both the sparse and the single-axis reconstructions reproduce the chiral stripe pattern of the full dataset (Fig. 3b). A single tilt series rotates the sample about one axis, so the magnetization component along that axis never aligns with the beam and goes unmeasured (Sec. I). The physics prior fills it in: the micromagnetic energy couples the unmeasured component to the two measured ones, so all three come out consistent (red-framed panels in Fig. 3b). The two single-axis reconstructions differ mainly at the edge of the sample, where the disk mask fits worst, and noise differences between the two series slightly change the stripe pattern (Extended Data Fig. 2). 

However, agreement between the reduced and full reconstructions of the measured disk does not show that the unmeasured component is correct. We therefore repeat the four acquisitions on the synthetic disk, whose magnetization is known. At the weight of Fig. 3 ( _λ[E]_ = 10[15] ), the reconstruction recovers the three-dimensional helical winding in all three components for every acquisition, with _εm_ between 0 _._ 08 and 0 _._ 16 (Extended Data Fig. 1c), compared with 0 _._ 74 for the complete dataset without the physics prior. Consequently, a small fraction of the projections, or a single tilt series alone, suffices to recover the three-dimensional texture, so that vector tomography experiments become faster and can cover more experimental conditions.

Moreover, a parameter’s impact on the reconstruction matches its sensitivity in the readout: parameters that barely affect the texture cannot be extracted, but they also barely change the reconstructed state, whereas parameters that strongly shape the texture can be determined directly from the misfit. The sparse-data recovery and the parameter readout together suggest a strategy for experimental design. Dense dual-axis tilt series, typically required for highresolution three-dimensional imaging, take a significant amount of beamtime in vector tomography. With the physics prior, far fewer projections are required, and even a single tilt axis can provide a robust reconstruction. Every projection a reconstruction can spare buys beamtime for additional specimens, magnetic fields, or time points within one allocation, and thus for more complex experiments such as in situ studies. A single well-sampled measurement of a prepared reference state, such as a helical texture whose period pins the constants, could then validate the energy model and fix the material parameters, while subsequent conditions are imaged with sparse acquisitions. 

We demonstrate this physics-informed approach with XMCD tomography, but automatic differentiation in micromagnetic software [28, 35] allows the physics prior to be paired with any differentiable forward model and extended with further interactions of the host material. The same approach can therefore be extended to other magnetic imaging modalities, including electron holographic and Lorentz tomography [12, 13] and laminography [11].
