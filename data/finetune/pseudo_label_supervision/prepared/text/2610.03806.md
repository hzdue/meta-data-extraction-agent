Sodium chloride is a major component of extraterrestrial oceans, but it is generally assumed to be excluded from the high-pressure ice mantles of water-rich planets. Here we show that a concentrated NaCl solution, when quenched in an amorphous state before compression, crystallises in a metastable NaCl-bearing ice VII phase. Synchrotron x-ray diffraction and neutron diffraction reveals an expanded and strongly disordered ice VII lattice with no detectable crystalline NaCl. _Ab initio_ calculations reproduce lattice expansion by substituting water molecules for Na[+] and Cl _[−]_ ions and show that the structural response increases strongly with salt concentration. Upon decompression at 300 K, the phase expels crystalline NaCl at 5 _._ 1 _±_ 0 _._ 3 GPa and does not reform upon recompression, demonstrating that its formation is controlled by the thermodynamic pathway. Despite an 8 _._ 5 % larger unit-cell volume, NaCl-bearing ice VII has a density close to that of pure ice VII and is substantially less dense than an equivalent mechanical mixture of ice and halite. These properties suggest that salty ice may be transported through deep convecting mantles but release salt near the 5 GPa boundary, where the denser products could inhibit further upward transport. Pathway-dependent salt trapping may therefore provide a previously unrecognised control on chemical exchange between rocky interiors and subsurface oceans. 

## **I. INTRODUCTION** 

Most models of icy-moon and water-rich-planet interiors start from a simple assumption: as high-pressure ice crystallises at depth, dissolved salts are pushed out into the liquid above. Here we show that this assumption fails for ice VII. Using a ‘quench-and-compress’ synthesis route, we demonstrate that NaCl can be extensively incorporated into the ice VII lattice as a genuine substitutional solid solution, with the local composition of the expanded phrase estimated to reach up to 33 _._ 1 wt%. The subsequent consequences of this new phase affect the understanding of how salt is distributed and how it moves inside icy worlds. 

The interiors of icy moons and water-rich planets are dominated by high-pressure phases of water ice, which control their density structure, internal stratification, and long-term thermal evolution [1–3]. Whether salts are retained within these deep ice phases or expelled into subsurface oceans is one of the least constrained parameters in current interior models, yet it has an outsized effect on ocean salinity, ice-shell density, and ultimately on habitability. be among the most abundant electrolytes in extraterrestrial oceans: it has been detected in NaCl-rich plume grains from Enceladus [4–7], inferred spectroscopically on Europa and Ganymede [8–11], and predicted in the geochemical models of ocean formation in icy bodies [6, 12– 14]. 

Despite this abundance, most interior models still assume NaCl is excluded from high-pressure ice polymorphs and partitions entirely into the liquid phase — an assumption with direct consequences for predicted density profiles, compositional layering, and convective transport within icy mantles. Whether salts can instead be incorporated into the dense ice phases stable at several gigapascals — pressures typical of the deep interiors of large icy moons such as Ganymede and Titan [15, 16] — has remained an open question. 

At low pressures ( _<_ 0 _._ 3 GPa), the answer seems clear: NaCl is consistently rejected upon freezing [17, 18], and the only known crystalline hydrate, hydrohalite (NaCl _·_ 2 H2O), is stable only near ambient pressure and low temperature [19, 20]. Even here, though, the picture is more complex than a simple binary of ‘excluded or not’. Rapid freezing of NaCl brine at ambient pressure can produce metastable and amorphous NaCl – H2O phases beyond hydrohalite, with the resulting solid-phase assemblage recording the cooling history of the parent fluid [21]. At moderate pressures (< 2 GPa) and low

temperature (150–300 K), hyperhydrated phases such as 2 NaCl _·_ 17 H2O (SC8.5) and NaCl _·_ 13 H2O (SC13) [22] show that NaCl can be stoichiometrically incorporated into a water-ice framework [22]. SC13 even adopts an ice-VI-like hydrogen-bond network stabilised by ionic interactions [23]. But these phases destabilise near the ice VI–ice VII transition ( _∼_ 2 _._ 2 GPa) [22], leaving the fate of NaCl in deeper ice VII mantles unknown. 

Above this transition, the response of ice VII to dissolved NaCl turns out to depend strongly on both concentration and thermodynamic path [24, 25]. At low concentrations (1–5 wt%), ice VII forms a homogeneous phase with a slightly contracted lattice relative to the pure system [26–28]. This behaviour is consistent with ions acting as isolated point defects, locally distorting the hydrogen-bond network without altering the global structure. At elevated temperatures ( _>_ 500 K), this dilute regime breaks down via halite precipitation, suggesting limited long-term stability of dissolved salt under some conditions [27]. Computational studies suggest small amounts of NaCl can nonetheless be accommodated substitutionally within the ice VII lattice, extending the superionic regime and enabling ionic transport [29], while spectroscopy reveals shifts in the hydrogen-bond symmetrisation transition in its presence [30]. None of this, however, tells us what happens at higher concentrations, or whether a qualitatively different, collective incorporation regime exists and which structure is formed. 

A clue comes from the LiCl – H2O system. In LiClbearing ice VII, rapid quenching ( _∼_ 10[2] Ks _[−]_[1] ) of concentrated solutions followed by compression above _∼_ 4 GPa — a route we refer to hereafter as the ‘quench-andcompress’ method — produces a distinct salty ice VII phase with an expanded lattice and suppressed proton ordering [31, 32]. This pathway relies on the formation of an amorphous precursor in which the ions locally achieve the coordination environment they will eventually need in the crystal [25]. Crystallisation can then proceed through local relaxation around these pre-configured environments, rather than requiring a collective reorganisation of the whole structure after the fact. For NaCl in ice VII, both Na[+] and Cl[–] require eightfold coordination, reached only at higher pressures ( _∼_ 5 GPa) [24, 25] than for LiCl-bearing ice VII. Whether the same quenchand-compress pathway (Fig. 1) can stabilise a true NaCl substitutional solid solution in ice VII, and whether doing so drives a transition from defect-like incorporation to collective structural modification, has remained unresolved until now. 

Here we demonstrate that a large amount of NaCl can be extensively incorporated into ice VII as a substitutional solid solution, metastable above 5 _._ 1 _±_ 0 _._ 3 GPa at 300 K. Combining synchrotron X-ray diffraction, neutron scattering, and _ab initio_ calculations, we identify a previously unknown NaCl-bearing ice VII phase with a unit cell 8 _._ 5 % larger than pure ice VII and no detectable crystalline NaCl anywhere in the sample. ## **II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS AND MATERIALS** 

## **A. Sample Preparation** 

For X-Ray diffraction (XRD) experiments, a eutectic NaCl solution (25 wt%, NaCl _·_ 9.7 H2O) was prepared using `Milli-Q` water with 99 _._ 99 % purity and NaCl from `Sigma-Aldrich` with a purity _≥_ 99 _._ 5 %. The solution was both prepared and loaded into a membrane Diamond Anvil Cell (DAC) under a dry Nitrogen atmosphere. 

Type _IIas_ diamond anvils with culet diameters of 250 and 500 µm were used in our DACs. Rhenium-foil gaskets were used to contain the sample whilst ruby and gold spheres were used as pressure calibrants [33–35]. The equation of state of ice VII [36–38] was also used as an additional pressure calibrant for the XRD experiments. The sample was formed by following the quenchand-compress thermodynamic path as shown in Fig. 1, and described previously. The sample was compressed to 0 _._ 3 GPa before the entire DAC was plunged into a bath of liquid Nitrogen (77 K), quenching the solution to form an amorphous precursor. At this cryogenic temperature, the sample was compressed to the target pressure, between 6 _−_ 10 GPa before then being warmed up to room temperature. The pressure was carefully monitored upon warming to ensure that the sample remained in the target pressure range of above 6 GPa. 

precursor was prepared using a high-pressure sprayinjection technique as described by Ludl _et al._ [43]. A solution of 50 mm[3] of NaCl _·_ 10.2 D2O was sprayed into an evacuated chamber (order of 1 mbar) and deposited onto a plate which was kept at _∼_ 80 K, enabling rapid vitrification and retention of the dissolved salt. Sample quality was checked using the XRD platform at the Institut de Minéralogie, de Physique des Matériaux et de Cosmochimie (IMPMC, Paris, France), with the sample under vacuum and at 77 K. Once the amorphous precursor was confirmed, it was stored in liquid nitrogen so the NaCl would not crystallise out of the amorphous. The sample had to be prepared in this way since the Paris-Edinburgh (PE) press used in high-pressure neutron diffraction requires several tens of mm[3] of sample. On site, the amorphous precursor was cryogenically loaded (80 K) into a PE high-pressure clamp with cubic Boron Nitride (cBN) anvils[44], along with a piece of lead (Pb) acting as a pressure calibrant [45, 46] It was then placed into the PE press and compressed to the target pressure (6 _−_ 10 GPa), all whilst at 80 K. The entire press and sample were then allowed to warm up to ambient temperature. This way, we followed the quench-andcompress thermodynamic pathway as shown in Fig. 1. Measurements were done using a wavelength of 1 _._ 54 or 2 _._ 41 Å, depending on the desired resolution. Pressure was determined from the equation of state of Pb [46] and ice VII [36]. Subsequent data refinement was carried out using the `FullProf Suite` [42]. 

## **B. X-Ray Diffraction** 

## **D. Computational Details** 

Angle-dispersive X-ray powder diffraction patterns were acquired at both the ID15b and ID27 beamlines at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF, Grenoble, France) using a monochromatic X-ray beam ( _λ_ = 0.4096 Å for ID15b, _λ_ = 0.3738 Å for ID27) and an `Eiger2 9M` CdTe flat-panel detector. A typical acquisition time of 10 s [39, 40] was used. The beam spot size for both instruments was _∼_ 6 _×_ 6 µm, and the DAC was rocked 5 _−_ 10 ° during the acquisition. The 2D diffraction patterns were treated in `Dioptas` [41] to mask the Bragg peaks of the diamond anvils, then integrated into powderaveraged one-dimensional patterns, and a smooth polynomial background was subtracted. Subsequent Le Bail fittings were performed using the `FullProf Suite` [42]. In the XRD measurements, the pressure was determined using the equation of state of gold [35], the equation of state of ice VII [36, 38] or ruby fluorescence [33, 34], depending on the experimental conditions. Where multiple calibrants were available, the resulting pressures were cross-checked to ensure consistency.

## **III. RESULTS** 

## **A. Discovery of a NaCl-bearing ice VII phase** 

Following the quench-and-compress path (Fig. 1) with the 25 wt% NaCl solution and subsequent warming to room temperature, synchrotron XRD at the ID27 beamline (ESRF) revealed a previously unobserved, expanded ice VII phase coexisting with pure ice VII at 7 _._ 67 GPa, as determined by temperature-corrected ruby fluorescence [34, 55]. Both phases adopt the cubic _Pn_ 3 _m_ space 

group but have distinct unit-cell parameters (Fig. 2b). One gives complete Debye–Scherrer rings, consistent with pure ice VII whilst the other gives partially ordered rings, suggesting uniaxial strain within the crystal. Critically, mapping the full sample volume, no crystalline NaCl, nor any NaCl-hydrate, was detected. Together with the expanded ice VII lattice and the agreement with the substitutional calculations, this observation indicates extensive incorporation of Na[+] and Cl _[−]_ into the ice VII structure. 

A Le Bail fitting scheme was used to determine the unit-cell and peak-profile parameters of the two ice VII phases without imposing a detailed atomic model. These refinements show that the NaCl-bearing ice VII phase has a unit cell _∼_ 8 _._ 5 % larger than the pure, coexisting ice VII. The refinement further also shows that the diffraction peaks of the NaCl-bearing ice VII phase are 4 _._ 5 times broader than those of the pure ice VII, indicating substantially more heterogeneous microstructure in the expanded phase, discussed further in Section IV.B. 

A separate constrained Rietveld refinement of this ID27 diffraction data was used for the limited purpose of estimating the oxygen isotropic displacement parame2 ter. The refined value increased from _B_ iso = 1 _._ 05(2) Å 2 in the coexisting pure ice VII phase to 11 _._ 8(9) Å in the NaCl-bearing ice VII phase. Rietveld refinement was not used to determine site occupancies or a detailed atomic arrangement. Hydrogen positions and their displacement parameters were not refined because of the limited sensitivity of X-ray diffraction to hydrogen. Given the substantial substitutional disorder and the correlations between displacement, occupancy and profile parameters, the increased oxygen _B_ iso should therefore be regarded as an effective indicator of enhanced structural disorder rather than as a quantitative measure of atomic thermal motion. 

The expanded lattice, together with the absence of crystalline NaCl or any NaCl-hydrate, indicates that NaCl is incorporated within the ice VII structure itself rather than forming a separate phase. 

It is important to note though that the relative proportions of pure and expanded ice VII differ between samples prepared using different DAC geometries. DACs with diamonds with a 500 µm culets and another with culets of 250 µm were both used. Both samples were prepared with the same 25 wt% NaCl starting solution. The sample within the DAC with 500 µm culets contained a greater proportion of pure ice VII than the sample prepared with the DAC with 250 µm (See Fig. 2). Analysis of the relative XRD intensities, corrected for the scattering-power factors of the two phases, indicates that the local NaCl-bearing ice VII concentrations are approximately 26 _._ 9 wt% (NaCl _·_ 8.82 H2O, Fig. 2a) for the DAC with 250 µm diamonds and 33 _._ 1 wt% (NaCl _·_ 6.56 H2O, Fig. 2b) in the DAC with 500 µm culets. Additional experimental support of the NaCl-bearing ice VII was obtained from neutron diffraction measurements at the D20 beamline at the Institut Laue-Langevin (ILL), Grenoble, France. The amorphous precursor was produced and cryoloaded as described in the methods section II.C. Measurements were taken whilst the amorphous was warmed to form crystalline ice VII (Supplementary Fig.S1). The resulting neutron diffraction patterns collected at room temperature and shown in Fig.S2, again reveal two cubic lattices and no crystalline NaCl. However, in this case, the expanded ice VII unit cell volume is greater than that of pure ice VII by only _∼_ 3 %. This expansion is significantly smaller than the previously mentioned XRD experiments. During the sprayquenching process small, cubic crystalline inclusions were observed to have formed, but not collected, as their identity was uncertain at the time. Since a reduced lattice expansion was measured with neutrons, it is plausible that these crystallites corresponded to crystalline NaCl which had separated during the spray-quenching process. If salt segregation during quenching had occurred, the effective NaCl concentration incorporated into the amorphous precursor, and thus into the ice VII lattice, would have been lower than the original solution concentration, resulting in a smaller expansion and placing the sample into the dilute regime. 

Na[+] and Cl[–] pairs within the ice VII supercell prior to relaxation. The 19 wt% composition is used as a representative case for discussing local structure. Configurations in which Na–Cl distances approached 2 _._ 7 Å — the Na–Cl distance in crystalline NaCl at these pressures [46] — were excluded. Their simulated diffraction patterns show strong low-angle Bragg reflections that are absent from the experimental data, strongly disfavouring configurations containing substantial Na – Cl pairing, resembling embryonic NaCl. The remaining, non-ionpaired configurations preserve the ice VII topology, despite Na[+] and Cl _[−]_ each substituting for a water molecule (Fig. 3). These structures show only weak low-angle reflections, which diminish further with increasing disorder, consistent with the broadened, largely featureless diffraction observed experimentally. The local hydrogen-bond network reorganises around each ion. The hydrogens of the water molecules orientate toward neighbouring Cl[–] , while oxygen atoms orientate towards Na[+] . The remaining water network preserves the Bernal–Fowler ice rules away from the substituted sites. The model is consistent with the experimental diffraction observations and provides a structural mechanism by which the ice framework accommodates the incorporated ions.

## **IV. DISCUSSION** 

## **A. Pathway- and concentration-dependent incorporation** 

The key distinction between the NaCl-bearing ice VII identified here and previously reported dilute salty ice VII lies not only in salt concentration, but also in the pathway by which crystallisation occurs. Direct compression of aqueous NaCl solutions (‘Direct compression’ pathway, Fig. 1), as in our XRD experiments (Fig. S3) and previous work [26], produces ice VII without the pronounced lattice expansion obtained through the quenchand-compress route. A possible explanation is that crystallisation during direct compression precedes the development of the local ionic coordination favourable for extensive substitution. Rapid quenching instead traps the ions within an amorphous H2O matrix, which can be 

8 

compressed before crystallisation. At pressures above approximately 5 GPa this pathway favours the development of eightfold ionic coordination [25], allowing the ice VII network to crystallise around preconfigured ionic environments during subsequent warming. 

This interpretation accounts for the strong pathway dependence and the failure of the salt-bearing phase to reform after desalting. Once NaCl exsolves and crystallises as halite upon decompression, simply increasing the pressure does not restore the dispersed ionic environments present in the compressed amorphous precursor. The observed irreversibility therefore highlights a substantial kinetic barrier to reincorporation. The metastable retention of the expanded phase above (5 _._ 1 _±_ 0 _._ 3) GPa at 300 K should consequently be distinguished from an equilibrium solubility boundary. We stress that the desalting pressure of 5 _._ 1 _±_ 0 _._ 3 GPa is the experimentally determined kinetic desalting threshold at 300 K, rather than a universal equilibrium boundary. Its location under planetary geotherms remains to be established. 

Salt concentration provides a second control on the structural response. Fig. 7 compares the calculated lattice expansion at approximately 6, 19 and 35 wt% NaCl with the experimental result at approximately 26 _._ 9 wt%, showing an increasing expansion with salt content. At low concentration, individual ions can be viewed as isolated defects: they reorientate neighbouring water molecules and perturb local O–O distances, while the surrounding hydrogen-bond network accommodates these distortions. This picture is consistent with the small lattice changes observed in dilute systems, including our neutron diffraction experiment (Fig. S2). Related local ionic distortions have also been reported in the ice-VI-like SC13 framework [23]. At higher concentrations, the distorted environments surrounding neighbouring ions are expected to overlap, producing a collective perturbation of the water network rather than independent local relaxations. Analogous effects have been discussed for concentrated ionic solutions [57, 58]. 

The pronounced lattice expansion at high NaCl content may thus reflect a crossover from isolated local distortions to a more collective response of the hydrogenbond network, while preserving the average ice VII topology. A systematic experimental study as a function of salt concentration is needed to test this interpretation and establish how the structural response evolves between the dilute and concentrated limits. These results nevertheless highlight the importance of both composition and crystallisation history in determining how highpressure ice accommodates salt.

For the composition investigated here, the increase in unit-cell volume largely compensates for the replacement of water molecules by the heavier Na[+] and Cl[–] ions. ## **C. Implications for planetary interior dynamics and salt transport** 

The contrast between incorporated and exsolved salt has direct implications for transport through highpressure ice mantles. Hernandez et al. [29] predicted NaCl-bearing ice VII to be denser than pure ice VII and comparable in density to the corresponding assemblage of ice VII and crystalline salt. Even under those conditions, their simulations showed that NaCl-bearing ice VII could be efficiently entrained by mantle convection. Our results strengthen this conclusion by showing that the density of the substitutional phase is instead close to that of pure ice VII (Fig. 6), implying only a limited compositional buoyancy contrast during transport through the deep mantle. 

This density behaviour has two main consequences for salt transport. First, NaCl-bearing ice VII formed or retained in the deep mantle could be transported upwards by solid-state convection without a major density penalty. Second, once the upwelling material crosses the 5 _._ 1 _±_ 0 _._ 3 GPa desalting boundary, NaCl exsolves from the ice VII lattice and forms a substantially denser ice–halite assemblage. If the exsolved salt remains mechanically coupled to the surrounding ice, this increase in density could reduce the buoyancy of the upwelling material and favour partial downward recycling. Alternatively, segregation of crystalline halite could promote the development of a NaCl-rich layer near the transition. In either case, desalting would tend to inhibit, or at least reduce, the continued upward transport of salt towards the overlying ocean. 

The desalting transition may therefore act as a pressure-controlled compositional filter, regulating not only how rapidly salt is transported but also how much of it can ultimately reach the surface ocean. Its efficiency will depend on the competition between convective entrainment, phase separation and gravitational recycling. Detailed numerical modelling will be required to determine what fraction of the exsolved NaCl continues upwards and what fraction accumulates near the transition or is recycled into the deeper mantle. 

tion of the high-pressure ice mantle lies at pressures above this threshold. For massive water-rich planets ( _>_ 2 _ME_ ), most of the high-pressure ice mantle may therefore lie within the pressure range in which NaCl-bearing ice VII is metastably retained at 300 K. In this sense, a pressureinduced desalting transition could act as a compositional filter analogous to a differentiation boundary, controlling not simply the rate of salt transport but the fraction of salt that ultimately reaches the surface ocean. Quantifying this effect under realistic planetary geotherms will require constraining the temperature and concentration dependence of the desalting transition. 

Several open questions remain. The concentrations studied here (26 _._ 9 wt%) far exceed realistic ocean salinities, but they could be representative of salt-rich brine zones developed at the ice/rock interface as a result of leaching processes [2]. Experiments at lower concentrations are needed to establish whether the substitutional regime and its volume expansion persist at various values of salt concentration. The high-temperature stability of the phase is unresolved as well. Frank _et al._ observed halite exsolution above 500 K from lowconcentration salty ice VII, but whether this applies to the structurally distinct substitutional phase is unknown. The behaviour of other geochemically relevant electrolytes, including KCl, MgCl2 and CaCl2, in ice VII also remains unexplored. 

Despite these open questions, the present results demonstrate that high-pressure ice cannot always be treated as a passive, salt-excluding layer. The structural form in which salt is stored, and the pathway by which that structure forms, can substantially alter the density of the resulting assemblage and its response to decompression. Pathway-dependent incorporation and pressure-induced desalting may therefore provide a previously unrecognised control on chemical exchange between rocky interiors and subsurface oceans.

## **I. SUPPLEMENTARY RESULTS** 

## **A. Neutron Diffraction** 

Additional experimental support for the concentration dependence of the expansion of the NaCl-bearing ice VII was obtained from neutron diffraction measurements at the D20 beamline at the Institut Laue-Langevin (ILL), Grenoble, France. Neutron high-pressure measurements require the use of a Paris-Edinburgh (PE) Press which requires sample sizes in the order of _∼_ mm[3] . Therefore, the sample was produced and loaded, as described in the main text. 

The resulting amorphous material was cryoloaded into the PE press, compressed, and subsequently warmed to form crystalline ice VII (Supplementary Fig. S1). 

The resulting neutron diffraction patterns collected at room temperature and shown in Fig. S2, again reveal two cubic lattices. However, in this case, the expanded ice VII unit cell volume is greater than that of pure ice VII by only _∼_ 1–2 %. This expansion is significantly smaller than that observed in the higher-concentration samples described in the main text. 

During the spray-quenching process small, cubic crystalline inclusions were observed to have formed, but not collected, as their identity was uncertain at the time. Now that we see that there is a reduced lattice expansion measured with neutrons, it is plausible that these crystallites corresponded to crystalline NaCl which had separated during the spray-quenching process. If partial desalting had occurred, the effective NaCl concentration incorporated into the amorphous precursor, and thus into the ice VII lattice, would have been lower than the original solution concentration, resulting in a smaller expansion. Using Fig.7 in the main text, we can determine that the observed expanded ice VII with an expansion of _∼_ 1–2 % corresponds to a salt concentration of _∼_ 5 wt% of NaCl. 

## _1. Refinement Details_ 

Neutron diffraction data and X-diffraction data were treated with the `FullProf` Suite [1]. A Le Bail fitting scheme was utilised, to reliably obtain unit cell and peak-profile parameters for the Equation of State (Fig.5) and desalting pressure (Fig.4) analyses. Constrained Rietveld Refinements were used to determine the B _iso_ values from the XRD data (Fig.2a and b), and as reported in section III.A. Here, all the parameters of the refinement were refined oneby-one before B _iso_ was refined last. Whilst refining B _iso_ , the peak-shape parameter (U) was held fixed to reduce correlations with B _iso_ . Although the value of B _iso_ remained sensitive to parameter correlations, the substantially larger B _iso_ of the NaCl-bearing phase compared to pure ice VII was robust across the refinement strategies.

## **B. Room Temperature Compression Results** 

Final support for thermodynamic pathway dependence, and concentration dependent expansion of the NaCl-bearing ice VII was obtained at the Psiché beamline at Soleil, Essonne, France. Here, a DAC was loaded with a salty aqueous solution with a NaCl concentration of 26 wt%. The cell was quickly compressed from the liquid phase and into the solid crystalline phase to 3 _._ 82 GPa. A pure ice VII phase, a slightly expanded ice VII and the pure crystalline NaCl phase were observed via XRD in the centre of the cell at 44 _._ 23 GPa. The two peaks from the pure ice VII and slightly expanded ice VII were overlapping at low pressure, but due to the different bulk modulii of the phases, separated further at higher pressure. Fig. S3 shows the evolution of the sample as it was compressed up to 44 _._ 23 GPa at 300 K. 

These results demonstrate that even upon rapid direct compression, without the ‘quench-and-compress’ thermodynamic pathway, a small fraction of NaCl can be incorporated into the ice VII lattice, producing a measurably expanded unit cell at higher pressures. However, the incorporation is incomplete. A large amount of crystalline NaCl is clearly observed, and the expansion of _∼_ 3 %, corresponding to _∼_ 8 wt% uptake (from Le Bail refinement) is substantially smaller than in samples prepared via the ‘quench-and-compress’ pathway. This confirms that the thermodynamic pathway controls both the efficiency of incorporation and the resulting lattice expansion. We also note

## **C. Thermal Correction of Calculations for NaCl-bearing ice VII**
