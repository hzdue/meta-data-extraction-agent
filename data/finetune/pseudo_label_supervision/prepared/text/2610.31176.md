Abstract. Abnormal grain growth is inherently three-dimensional, making 3D tracking of grain evolution essential for understanding its underlying mechanisms. This study combines synchrotron-based diffraction-contrast tomography and phase-contrast tomography to characterize the three-dimensional grain structure and quantify the size and spatial distribution of second-phase particles in the aluminum alloy AA5252. Using an interrupted annealing protocol, we tracked the persistent growth of abnormal grains and the emergence of abnormal growth in a grain initially belonging to the matrix. Although second-phase particles effectively suppressed grain growth in the matrix, variations in local particle density showed no apparent correlation with either the emergence or subsequent rapid growth of abnormal grains. Instead, crystallographic misorientation and, more notably, neighboring grain size were identified as stronger predictors of abnormal growth rate. Matrix grains were consumed more rapidly when they were relatively small and shared high-angle grain boundaries with the growing abnormal grain. The same trends were observed for the grain that underwent a transition from normal to abnormal growth. Remarkably, even before the onset of abnormal growth, this grain was surrounded by smaller matrix grains with exclusively high-angle grain boundaries, while the local particle density was slightly higher than average. These findings suggest that the same microstructural characteristics facilitate both the emergence and persistence of abnormal grain growth, although the mechanism triggering the transition to rapid growth remains unresolved.

## 1. **Introduction** 

Heating a polycrystalline material almost inevitably leads to coarsening of its underlying microstructure. In single-phase materials, the mechanism of coarsening is referred to as grain growth, and when this process proceeds in a more-or-less uniform manner, it is called normal grain growth. Under certain experimental conditions, normal grain growth can be suppressed such that only a subset of grains in the polycrystal is able to grow. These grains rapidly consume their slower-growing or completely stagnant neighbors (the grains making up the “matrix”), and the resulting growth mode is known as abnormal grain growth (AGG). 

AGG is associated with a transition from a unimodal to a bimodal grain size distribution (GSD) [1,2]. Such bimodality can significantly alter the properties of crystalline materials, i.e., most metals and ceramics. Depending on the application, AGG may be desired, but usually it is not. For example, in grain-oriented silicon steels [3], AGG helps to develop a magnetically preferred orientation, which reduces core losses in electrical transformers. On the other hand, AGG is generally undesirable in steels, as it leads to a heterogeneous microstructure characterized by a small number of excessively large grains [4]. Such microstructural heterogeneity can compromise mechanical performance, as demonstrated in friction-stir-welded low-carbon steel, where AGG has been associated with reduced hardness and tensile strength [5]. Similar detrimental effects have been reported in other engineering alloys. In the nickel-based superalloy Inconel 718, which is widely used in aerospace applications due to its high strength and excellent fatigue resistance, AGG has been shown to significantly reduce fatigue life [6]. Beyond its effects on bulk mechanical properties, AGG can also impair surface quality. In 6xxx-series aluminum alloys used for lightweight automotive body panels, the formation of a few abnormally large grains within a finer-grained matrix can promote surface defects such as roping [7]. 

Phenomenologically, we know that, in order for AGG to occur, a certain number of grains must first obtain and then somehow retain a growth advantage over crystallites in the surrounding matrix. Any proposed mechanism for AGG, therefore, must be able to explain an abnormal grain’s _emergence_ —the moment it begins outgrowing its neighbors—as well as its _persistence_ in growing faster. (Here, we prefer to use emergence rather than nucleation to avoid confusion with phenomena such as recrystallization or phase transformations, for which nucleation is associated with an energy barrier.) 

Of the many mechanisms for AGG mentioned in the literature [8], the two most commonly cited are related to the presence of texture [2,9,10] and pinning particles [11,12]. In textured materials, AGG can occur when a few grains deviate from the main component of crystallographic orientation in the specimen [2]. Because high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) typically have higher energies [13] and mobilities [14] than low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs), any aboveaverage-sized misaligned grain that forms HAGBs with matrix grains belonging to the main component of crystallographic orientation can outgrow the surrounding matrix, whose grains are predominantly separated by LAGBs owing to the crystallographic texture. Similarly, AGG can also be observed in materials containing small, second-phase particles that retard or even completely prevent the migration of grain boundaries through Zener pinning [15–17]. According to Humphreys’ model for AGG [18], the largest grains in a pinned microstructure may continue to grow as long as they exceed their neighbors in size by a sufficiently large ratio, whereby the exact value of this threshold depends on the size and volume fraction of the pinning particles. In this scenario, persistence follows from the driving force for grain growth being able to overcome the particle pinning pressure whenever a sufficiently large grain comes into contact with a smaller matrix grain [18]. The growth advantage is maintained in this mechanism by the fact that, as a large grain grows, its size ratio relative to the matrix steadily increases, provided the matrix grains remain (nearly) pinned. 

While AGG has long been studied in two-dimensional (2D) images of microstructures, only a handful of time-resolved three-dimensional (3D) measurements have appeared to date in the literature [19–23]. This scarcity persists despite the growing availability of nondestructive 3D 

2 

imaging techniques [24–30]. Because AGG is an inherently 3D phenomenon, such measurements are essential to capture the emergence of abnormal grains and to gain deeper insight into the mechanisms driving their persistent growth during prolonged annealing. 

The proper characterization of these dynamics, however, is complicated by certain aspects of AGG itself. Even after conditions promoting AGG have been established, abnormal grains may fail to form within a given region under investigation, or their growth may proceed too quickly between annealing steps to reveal the trajectory taken by the abnormal grain through the matrix, obscuring the mechanisms involved. Moreover, technical difficulties in reconstructing polycrystalline volumes with bimodal grain size distributions often lead to an inaccurate determination of grain boundary (GB) locations precisely where the latter are most relevant to AGG: namely, at the interfaces between abnormal and matrix grains. The aforementioned challenges are even more pronounced in materials exhibiting extreme forms of AGG, during which abnormal grains grow to sizes at least an order of magnitude larger than the mean size of the matrix grains [8]. Conversely, it is precisely these materials that enable us to focus on the clearest cases of AGG, where one might expect the underlying mechanism(s) to be easiest to detect. 

In light of these considerations, we employed synchrotron-based diffraction-contrast tomography (DCT) at beamline ID11 of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) to map a representative volume of the commercial aluminum alloy 5252 (AA5252) following sequential discrete (15-minute) annealing steps. This material is known to exhibit extreme AGG in a certain range of annealing temperatures, thus raising the likelihood of detecting incipient AGG and capturing the subsequent persistent growth of individual abnormal grains. DCT is capable of delivering high-resolution mappings of polycrystalline microstructures [31, 32], even for fine-grained materials, which enables individual GBs to be located and tracked during annealing-induced migration. Furthermore, the high spatial coherence of the x-ray beam at synchrotron sources makes phase-contrast tomography (PCT) [33] feasible, by which second-phase particles—known to be present in AA5252—can be identified, even when their absorption contrast is too low for conventional absorption-based computed tomography (CT). By fusing DCT and PCT datasets, we can examine how grain neighborhood relationships, GB character (in particular, crystallographic misorientation) and second-phase particles influence the emergence and persistence of AGG in AA5252. 

## 2. **Material and methods** 

Selecting a material that exhibits AGG and can be annealed in ambient air simplifies microstructure characterization. We therefore chose the commercial aluminum alloy AA5252, which, based on preliminary measurements, manifests AGG at annealing temperatures around 500 _[◦]_ C. This alloy is closely related to AA5052, which has a comparable chemical composition within the Al–Mg (5xxx) series and has been studied extensively with regard to AGG [34–37]. The larger mean grain size of recrystallized AA5252, however, improves the reliability of 3D characterization by DCT and was an important factor in selecting this alloy. In both alloys, the alloying elements promote the formation of second-phase particles [38,39], providing an opportunity to investigate their role in particle-mediated AGG [12]. 

## 2.1. **Sample preparation and data acquisition** 

A cylindrical specimen with a diameter of 0 _._ 8 mm was prepared by wire electrical discharge machining of a cold-rolled AA5252 sheet (80% thickness reduction), which was supplied by Constellium and rolled at the Centre for Material Forming (CEMEF). Magnesium is the primary alloying element in 5xxx series aluminum alloys, and our sample contained approximately 2.7 wt% Mg and 0.4 wt% Mn, together with smaller amounts of Fe, Si, Zn and Cu. While Zn and Cu remain largely in solid solution, Mn, Fe and Si tend to form intermetallic precipitates. The 

3 

larger of these second-phase particles were found to be iron-rich, as revealed by energy-dispersive spectroscopy during our initial characterization of the material, consistent with previous reports in the literature [39]. 

The cylindrical sample was affixed to an alumina rod with a ceramic adhesive and cured at room temperature for _>_ 2 h. The sample was then mounted on the rotation stage in the experimental hutch at beamline ID11 and recrystallized during two successive anneals of 30 min at 400 _[◦]_ C in a custom-built vertical furnace [40]. Subsequently, the specimen was annealed in 15-min intervals at 500 _[◦]_ C for a total of 90 min. The complete annealing and measurement protocol is depicted in supplementary Fig. S1. After each annealing step, the sample was cooled to room temperature and characterized by DCT and PCT. All measurements were performed with a monochromatic x-ray box beam at an energy of 43 _._ 66 keV. 

For DCT scans, diffraction spots were recorded using a FReLoN16 camera positioned 8 _._ 3 mm from the specimen, with an effective pixel side length of 1 _._ 78 µm. To reduce the number of simultaneously diffracting grains and thereby avoid excessive spot overlap on the DCT detector, we adjusted an aperture to limit the incident beam dimensions to 900 µm _×_ 380 µm (width _×_ height). After each annealing step, up to five adjacent scans were captured at vertical offsets of 350 µm, resulting in an overlap of 30 µm between scans. Each scan, hereafter referred to as a DCT layer, covered a full 360 _[◦]_ rotation at a constant angular step of 0 _._ 05 _[◦]_ , yielding 7200 diffraction images and a measurement time of about 25 min per DCT layer. 

Because PCT does not require grain indexing and is therefore not limited by the number of grains in the irradiated volume, we increased the incident beam size to 1300 µm _×_ 1300 µm. Two PCT scans with a vertical overlap of 300 µm were recorded at each annealing step. Images were acquired every 0 _._ 2 _[◦]_ of rotation for a total of 1800 projections and a scan time of 5 min per layer. The images were captured by an Andor Marana camera with an effective pixel side length of 0 _._ 61 µm, positioned 211 _._ 5 mm from the specimen. 

## 2.2. **Reconstruction of diffraction-contrast datasets** 

3D grain maps were reconstructed from the diffraction images with the DCT software package developed at ESRF based on Refs. [31,41]. Modifications had to be made, however, because the bimodal nature of the microstructure introduced unique challenges. Our specific reconstruction routine had to accommodate large abnormal grains as well as thousands of smaller matrix grains. 

In DCT, each diffraction spot corresponds to a 2D projection of the diffracting grain at a given sample rotation angle. Owing to the short sample-to-detector distance and the parallel beam optics, the size of each diffraction spot is directly related to the size of the diffracting grain [25]. Consequently, abnormally large grains produce very large diffraction spots on the detector, as exemplarily shown in supplementary Fig. S2. These are often truncated by the beam stop, or they may extend past the outer edges of the detector, resulting in inhomogeneous intensity distributions in the reconstructed grain volumes. These inhomogeneous intensity distributions make accurate segmentation challenging and lead to holes and overlaps within and around the abnormal grains in the assembled grain map. 

To compensate for the effects of diffraction spot truncation, we modified the reconstruction and segmentation procedures in two ways. First, diffraction spots that had been automatically discarded during the initial reconstruction because of truncation were re-evaluated by visual inspection. We manually included spots that were only moderately truncated and did not overlap with neighboring diffraction spots in the reconstruction. Including these additional diffraction spots provided additional projections and improved the reconstruction of the abnormal grains. Second, the segmentation threshold was reduced to account for the presence of low-intensity regions within the reconstructed abnormal grains. Because diffraction spot truncation reduces the reconstructed intensity, particularly near grain perimeters, a higher threshold would have 

4 

left these low-intensity regions unassigned. By adding projections and lowering the segmentation threshold, we obtained a more complete mapping of the abnormal grains, reducing both internal holes and gaps at the interface with surrounding matrix grains. The matrix grains, which did not suffer from truncation, were segmented with higher intensity thresholds. Overlapping regions between abnormal and matrix grains were then resolved by assigning disputed voxels to the grain with the higher reconstructed intensity. This criterion is applicable because the reconstructed intensities were normalized by grain volume, resulting in comparable intensity levels for abnormal and matrix grains. 

The large number of matrix grains within the illuminated sample volume results in numerous overlapping diffraction spots. This complicates the process of assigning them to unique grains and ultimately leads to ambiguities in the reconstructed grain maps, where multiple grains can extend over the same voxels. To resolve such ambiguities, we filtered the matrix grains based on various characteristics, including completeness and solidity, with the latter defined as the ratio of the grain volume to the volume of its convex hull and calculated using the `regionprops3` function within the Image Processing Toolbox of MATLAB. Completeness denotes the ratio of the number of experimentally detected diffraction spots arising from a reconstructed grain to the total number of diffraction spots calculated for that grain by forward projection [41,42]. A completeness of 1 indicates that all predicted diffraction spots were observed experimentally. A size-dependent threshold was applied to account for the generally lower completeness of smaller grains. Grains below a size-dependent completeness threshold (0.8 for the largest matrix grains, decreasing to 0.4 for the smallest) were excluded from the final grain map. Any remaining overlaps (disputed voxels) were then assigned to the grain with the higher reconstructed intensity at these positions in the grain map. 

## 2.3. **Reconstruction and segmentation of phase-contrast datasets** 

The projection images collected during the PCT scans were reconstructed on the Networked Interactive Computing Environment at ESRF using the tomography processing software Nabu [43] and Tomwer [44]. This workflow included background (flat-field) correction and phase retrieval using the Paganin algorithm [45]. The reconstruction was performed slice-wise, yielding a series of two-dimensional grayscale _x_ - _y_ slices. The resulting stack of slices constitutes the reconstructed grayscale volume. These reconstructed volumes exhibited a pronounced lowintensity artifact that appeared as a ring-like structure in the individual _x_ - _y_ slices (oriented perpendicular to the sample rotation axis), with both the intensity of the artifact and its appearance varying along the _z_ -direction. To reduce the effect of this artifact on subsequent segmentation, the grayscale intensity of each _x_ - _y_ reconstruction slice was normalized using the MATLAB function `imflatfield` . 

Despite this normalization, residual intensity inhomogeneities remained, making it infeasible to apply a single intensity threshold to segment all of the small, bright, second-phase particles. Instead, we employed the image-processing software Dragonfly [46] to leverage its machine learning-based segmentation capabilities. To that end, we trained a convolutional neural network (CNN): specifically, a UNet++ architecture with a depth level of 5 and 32 filters in the first network layer, iterated for 100 epochs using five representative _x_ - _y_ slices of the PCT reconstruction. In this procedure, several particles were manually selected from regions with different local grayscale intensities in the selected _x_ - _y_ -slices using the adaptive gaussian brush tool within Dragonfly. The segmentation targeted the upper intensity range using a constant sigma of 2 pixels and an intensity offset of 4 arbitrary units. To optimize local thresholding, the brush diameter—defining the localized neighborhood for background calculation—was dynamically adjusted between 3 µm and 14 µm. Pixels located within the marked areas were assigned to class 1, while all remaining pixels were automatically assigned to class 2. (The network requires two classes for training.) The CNN was then applied to the same _x_ - _y_ slices to automatically identify the particles. The resulting labeled images were visually examined, and additional 

5 

particles that were not identified by the network were manually annotated. These additional particles were incorporated into the training data, after which the CNN was retrained and reapplied to the selected slices. This iterative process was repeated until visual inspection indicated that the network reliably identified the majority of particles. The final trained CNN was subsequently applied to all _x_ - _y_ slices of the PCT reconstruction, yielding a three-dimensional segmentation of the second-phase particles. The same segmentation procedure was then applied to the complete time series of reconstructed PCT scans. 

## 2.4. **Registration of datasets** 

Because the DCT and PCT scans were captured with different detectors, the reconstructed grain maps and phase-contrast maps (including the segmented particles) were initially not aligned. As part of each DCT measurement, the DCT detector was used to record a complete projection series of low-quality absorption scans at the same effective pixel size as DCT. The resulting tomographic reconstruction contains many of the high-contrast features (such as edges, larger second-phase particles, etc.) that are also visible in the corresponding phase-contrast map. After identifying matching slices in both datasets, we downscaled the phase-contrast map from a voxel side length of 0 _._ 61 µm to 1 _._ 78 µm. 

Next, we registered the downscaled volume to the absorption volume (measured with the DCT detector) using a rigid-body transformation determined by the MATLAB function `imregtform` . We then applied the transformation to the centroids of the higher-resolution segmented particle map using the MATLAB function `transformPointsForward` , allowing them to be mapped onto the DCT grain map. 

In the as-reconstructed state, the DCT grain maps recorded at different annealing times were slightly misaligned, owing to slight displacements of the measurement setup (which likely occurred when the detectors were moved into and out of position during each measurement sequence). A pairwise registration of datasets from adjacent annealing intervals was performed by estimating and applying a rigid-body transformation to the extracted GB networks [47], starting with the grain map at 15 min. The same transformation matrices were then applied to the particle maps to ensure alignment between the grain and particle maps over the entire duration of annealing. 

## 2.5. **Grain tracking** 

After registration, a tracking algorithm [47, 48] was applied to the grain maps to ensure that grains have the same labels across all time steps. The algorithm uses two criteria to find matching grains: first, the fractional volume overlap and, second, the misorientation between pairs of grains in the current and previous time steps, tracking backward from later to earlier times. Grains with a minimum volume overlap of 25% and a maximum misorientation of 0 _._ 5 _[◦]_ in two adjacent time steps are stored as a match. If there are multiple matches for a given grain at the later time step, the pair with the largest volume overlap is stored as the final match. Using this procedure, 94% to 97% of all grains in a later dataset were successfully found in its immediately preceding dataset.

## 3.2. **Distribution and stability of pinning particles** 

While DCT provides information on the evolving grain structure, high-resolution PCT reveals the distribution of second-phase particles in the irradiated volume. Segmentation of the reconstructed PCT volumes yielded particle maps containing approximately 80 000 particles, with the smallest segmented particles measuring 0 _._ 76 µm in diameter, close to the effective voxel side length of 0 _._ 61 µm. The resulting particle count is roughly an order of magnitude greater than the number of grains identified within the same analyzed volume. This relatively high ratio indicates that a substantial number of potential pinning particles may interact with the grain boundary network. Three-dimensional visualization of the segmented particles (Fig. 4) reveals that they are not uniformly distributed throughout the volume but, rather, tend to align along the _z_ -direction, forming particle-rich columns or stringers. This morphology is perhaps not surprising, given that the _z_ -axis of the specimen coincides with the rolling direction of the sheet from which it was cut. 

The second-phase particles found by PCT are remarkably stable over the course of the experiment. Not only does the total number of particles detected remain nearly constant (within the uncertainty of the segmentation routine), but the mean size and spatial arrangement of the particles are also essentially unchanged. This conclusion is bolstered by direct comparison of the segmented particle maps at different annealing times. ## 4. **Discussion** 

The suppression of normal grain growth is a prerequisite for abnormal grain growth. In the aluminum alloy AA5252, this is apparently achieved by second-phase particles that cause growth to stagnate at a mean grain size of approximately 30 µm. These pinning particles are also remarkably stable—even at the high homologous annealing temperature of 500 _[◦]_ C, they neither coarsen nor dissolve, and although the particles create the stagnant matrix required for AGG, local variations in particle density do not appear to determine where abnormal growth proceeds. Thus, the central question becomes how boundaries of some grains are able to overcome essentially the same pinning conditions that arrest the surrounding matrix grains. More specifically, and in the sense of our investigation, we can ask: how does a matrix grain first escape the pinned population to emerge as an abnormal grain, and, once it has done so, what conditions facilitate the persistence of accelerated growth? 

Grain _G_ 59 provides a rare opportunity to address the question of emergence. Unlike _G_ 1, which was already substantially larger than the matrix grains at the beginning of the annealing experiment, _G_ 59 initially lies within the size range of matrix grains and begins to grow abnormally only after 45 min of annealing. Although _G_ 59 is larger than the mean matrix grain from the very beginning of the experiment, its size alone cannot explain its anomalous growth behavior, since many grains of comparable or even greater size do not undergo a transition to abnormal growth. The comparison in Fig. 6 with the 100 largest non-surface grains that remain part of the matrix provides a means of identifying other characteristics that may have favored the emergence of _G_ 59. (Only non-surface grains were considered in this comparison to minimize potential biases arising from reduced particle segmentation near specimen boundaries and from the different GB environments of grains intersecting a free surface.) Of the parameters examined, _G_ 59 is distinguished by an exclusively high-angle GB network and, perhaps most notably, by a small nearest-neighbor grain size. The significance of this result lies not simply in the preferential consumption of smaller grains, but in what grain size represents. Differences in grain size are associated with differences in the geometry and curvature of the surrounding GB network and, consequently, with differences in capillary driving pressure. The dependence of growth behavior on neighboring grain size therefore points toward variations in capillary driving force, rather than variations in local particle density, as an important factor governing continued abnormal grain boundary migration. The weaker dependence on misorientation further suggests that GB character modulates this response, since a sufficiently large curvature need not result in rapid migration if the energy or mobility of a particular GB is low.

The migration of boundary segments necessarily alters the geometry—and, thus, the driving pressure—of neighboring segments, which can, at the abnormal/matrix interface, provide additional pathways for abnormal growth to continue. Curvature alone, though, is insufficient to determine whether a GB migrates. For example, the strongly curved LAGB associated with the yellow-beige grain above _G_ 1 in Fig. 7 (b,c) remains nearly stationary over several annealing intervals. Whether or not a specific GB unpins and migrates also depends on GB-specific properties. Persistent abnormal growth can therefore be viewed as the result of a continuously evolving competition between capillary driving pressure and particle pinning, with GB character determining how readily a particular boundary responds. 

Viewed at the grain scale, this local picture is consistent with the mechanism proposed by Humphreys for persistent AGG in particle-pinned microstructures [18]. In his framework, a sufficiently large grain can continue to grow provided its size relative to the surrounding matrix remains above a threshold value set by the pinning-particle conditions. The preferential consumption of smaller nearest-neighbor grains that we observed for both _G_ 1 and _G_ 59 is consistent with this model and, in the context of curvature, provides an interpretation of the size-ratio criterion. Importantly, for persistence of this mechanism, the nearly stagnant matrix generates positive feedback once an abnormal grain has gained a sufficient size advantage. Further growth increases the size of the abnormal grain while the characteristic matrix grain size changes only marginally, so that the size disparity responsible for the growth advantage increases rather than diminishes. At the same time, consumption of neighboring grains continuously exposes the abnormal grain to new matrix grains and modifies the local geometry of its GBs. Wherever the resulting capillary driving pressure is sufficient to overcome particle pinning, the corresponding 

14 

GB will be sufficiently mobile, and migration can continue. In this way, the growth process itself reinforces the conditions that permit further abnormal growth. 

If this picture of particle-meidated AGG is correct, the distinction drawn between the _emergence_ and _persistence_ of an abnormal grain may be primarily phenomenological rather than mechanistic. At the scale of an individual GB, there is no fundamental difference between a boundary segment belonging to a grain that has not yet been classified as abnormal and one belonging to an already established abnormal grain. In both cases, migration is governed by the same underlying competition between capillary driving pressure and pinning pressure, modulated by GB-specific properties. During emergence, favorable local geometry and GB character must first facilitate sustained migration despite the pinning conditions that arrest most of the matrix. Thermally activated fluctuations of GBs may allow individual boundary segments to sample their local energy landscape and overcome pinning barriers when local conditions are sufficiently favorable. Once GB migration has given rise to a meaningful size advantage, the nearly stagnant matrix reinforces the size advantage criterion, thereby enabling continued growth to occur. Although the present experiment cannot resolve the exact event(s) that initiated this transition, the observations narrow the mechanistic problem considerably: rather than requiring abnormal grains to encounter systematically weaker particle pinning, the emergence and persistent growth of abnormal grains can be understood in terms of how local grain geometries and GB properties allow individual boundary segments to overcome the pinning field that keeps the surrounding matrix nearly stagnant. In a subsequent manuscript, we will apply the methods described in Ref. [53] to quantify the local GB curvature and migration kinetics at the abnormal/matrix interfaces of _G_ 1 and _G_ 59, thus enabling the balance between capillary driving pressure and particle pinning proposed here to be tested more directly. 

## 5. **Conclusions** 

By combining time-resolved diffraction-contrast and phase-contrast tomography, we investigated the emergence and persistent growth of abnormal grains in a commercial Al-Mg alloy (AA5252) containing finely dispersed second-phase particles. The principal findings are summarized as follows: 

1. Second-phase particles suppress normal grain growth but do not prevent abnormal grain growth. The particle population remained essentially unchanged during annealing, and the matrix grains exhibited very little growth, consistent with Zener pinning. Nevertheless, two abnormal grains were able to grow substantially at the expense of the surrounding matrix. 

2. Local variations in particle density do not appear to determine where abnormal grain growth occurs. Neither the emergence of an abnormal grain nor the preferential consumption of neighboring matrix grains was associated with locally reduced particle density. Thus, while particle pinning creates the conditions required for AGG, spatial variations in particle density do not appear to mediate its progression. 

3. Local grain geometry and grain boundary properties play an important role in abnormal growth. Smaller neighboring grains were consumed preferentially, while grain boundary misorientation exhibited a weaker but still discernible influence. These observations suggest that variations in local curvature and the associated capillary driving pressure, modulated by grain boundary properties, determine which boundaries can overcome particle pinning and continue to migrate. The resulting changes in boundary geometry near small grains that vanish may further reinforce abnormal growth. 

4. The emergence and persistence of abnormal grains may be governed by the same underlying mechanism. The observed emergence of abnormal grain _G_ 59 was associated with relatively small neighboring grains and an exclusively high-angle grain boundary network, rather than with reduced particle pinning. These findings suggest that favorable local grain geometry and grain 

15 

boundary properties can initiate abnormal growth, after which the increasing size advantage over the nearly stagnant matrix promotes continued AGG.

Figure S1. Complete annealing and measurement protocol for the AA5252 sample. After full recrystallization for 60 min at 400 _[◦]_ C, the sample was annealed in 15-minute steps at 500 _[◦]_ C for a total of 90 min. To cover a larger sample volume, the sample was characterized by five overlapping DCT scans and two overlapping PCT scans at room temperature after each annealing step.
