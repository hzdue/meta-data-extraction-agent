Altermagnets are novel magnetic systems that possess a spin-polarized electronic band structure without a net magnetic moment, making them promising for device applications. Hexagonal MnTe, a prototypical altermagnet, arguably exhibits the most properties consistent with theoretical predictions, including an anomalous Hall effect despite a vanishing net magnetization, and altermagnetinduced electronic band splitting. However, fundamental questions remain, including why some effects only appear significantly below the magnetic ordering temperature. Here, we resolve this discrepancy by revealing a reorientation of the Néel vector in single-crystalline MnTe. The Néel vector points 30 _[◦]_ from the _a_ -axis at low _T_ , before aligning directly with the _a_ -axis around _T[∗] ≃_ 260 K. We attribute this to single-ion anisotropy, which depends on temperature-dependent lattice parameters. We obtained these results using muon-spin spectroscopy, magnetization measurements, and X-ray diffraction; we show that the findings are consistent with neutron diffraction. Manipulating this effect, for example through strain, could unlock sensitive electronic detection schemes for external stimuli, paving the way for functional altermagnetic devices. 

Magnetically ordered solids are traditionally divided into two elementary phases: ferromagnets and antiferromagnets. A key characteristic of ferromagnets is a macroscopic magnetization due to the alignment of magnetic moments, which leads to a spin-polarized electronic structure and the anomalous Hall effect (AHE). Conversely, collinear antiferromagnets lack a net magnetization due to compensated magnetic order, yielding a spindegenerate band structure. Recently, altermagnetism, a third fundamental collinear magnetic phase that combines aspects of both other phases, was discovered [1]. 

Altermagnetism is characterised by a compensated collinear magnetic order in real space, specifically opposite spin sublattices connected by crystal rotation symmetries (symmorphic/non-symmorphic, proper/improper). In reciprocal space, unconventional spin-polarized bands with corresponding rotational symmetries manifest. This real-to-reciprocal-space correspondence produces electronic bands with broken time-reversal symmetry and alternating momentumdependent spin splitting [2]. Despite the compensated magnetic structure, these properties give rise to new phenomena, underpinning fundamental science and enabling robust spintronic memories, charge-spin conversion devices, and optospintronics [3].

Given these observations, detailed studies of the magnetism using various experimental probes are essential. To use MnTe in device applications, microscopic control of its domains is required [6]; thus, a complete picture of the realised magnetic structure(s) is crucial. Furthermore, surprising observations, such as the disappearance of the AHE [16, 17] and anisotropic magnetoresistance [5] well below the magnetic ordering temperature, hitherto lack explanation. Despite the technologically advantageous properties of MnTe, including a magnetic ordering temperature above room temperature and the ability to grow both single-crystal and thin-film samples, this knowledge gap limits research and hinders the realization of spintronic devices.

The results are shown in Fig. 4. 

As the magnetic moment rotates in the _ab_ -plane, the number of distinct internal fields at the muon site changes. Since we detect only two fields experimentally, the moments must point along one of two directions (and their symmetric equivalents): the _a_ -axis, or 30 _[◦]_ from the _a_ -axis, which we denote as the _a[∗]_ -axis. All other directions would yield three measured internal fields. For moments along the _a_ -axis, the calculated ratio between the two internal fields is just under 2, matching our observations above _T[∗]_ . Conversely, for moments along the _a[∗]_ - axis, the ratio is closer to 5, similar to what is observed at low _T_ . This indicates that below _T[∗]_ , the magnetic moments point 30 _[◦]_ from the _a_ -axis, along _a[∗]_ (matching the previously proposed magnetic structure [15]), before undergoing a spin reorientation transition at _T[∗]_ to align with the _a_ -axis.

A probable explanation is a temperature-dependent rotation or canting of the magnetic moments that yields a weak net magnetization, as suggested in Ref. [8]. Although the observation of a second magnetic phase is perhaps surprising, several other techniques have detected changes around _T[∗] ≃_ 260 K, supporting our findings. In micrometer-sized MnTe structures, Ref. [6] reports an AHE and X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) contrast at lower _T_ (150 K and 100 K, respectively), but both are absent at 250 K. Both the XMCD signal [6, 9] and the AHE [10, 17] are expected to depend strongly on the angle of the altermagnetic order vector and vanish completely when the moments point along the _a_ -direction [8], consistent with our observations. Other works [11, 12, 16] report significant changes in angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy and magnetotransport around _T ≃_ 260 K. While sometimes interpreted as the paramagnetic transition, this may be better explained by a change in the long-range magnetic order. 

Neutron scattering, often the gold-standard probe for such changes in magnetic structure, struggles to distinguish between moments along _a_ and _a[∗]_ . Spin reorientations typically manifest as intensity anomalies in Bragg reflections upon passing through the transition temperature. With equal magnetic domain populations, however, the structure factors produced by the _a_ and _a[∗]_ configurations are identical. Our neutron diffraction measurements [20] reveal no anomalies at _T[∗]_ , consistent with a 30 _[◦]_ spin reorientation in the _ab_ -plane assuming equally populated magnetic domains. 

To understand the mechanism driving the spin reorientation transition at _T[∗]_ , we turn to theory. Extensive density functional theory (DFT) work on MnTe shows that while a Hubbard _U_ is essential to stabilize the correct magnetic structure [21], spin-orbit coupling (SOC) has a negligible effect [22]. Furthermore, calculations of the magnetocrystalline anisotropy reveal very small energy differences for various in-plane moment orientations [5, 10, 15], supported experimentally by the ability to rotate the magnetic moments with an applied field [5]. This naturally arises from the 3d[5] ( _S_ = 5 _/_ 2, _L_ = 0) orbital filling of Mn[2+] in MnTe; the half-filled shell quenches the orbital angular momentum, minimizing spin-orbit coupling, which normally dominates magnetic anisotropy. Despite the complex magnetic exchange in MnTe [23], we argue that the magnetic anisotropy is instead driven by crystal-field effects and single-ion anisotropies.

Previously reported values for the anisotropy in MnTe [15, 25–27] vary between 20 and 250 _µ_ eV. This large range likely stems from different sample geometries (thin films vs. bulk), diverse experimental and theoretical approaches, and the inherent difficulty of measuring such a small effect. These values are consistent with our model, which expects small single-ion anisotropy. Despite this small energy scale, the consequences are significant; Refs. [5, 15] note that the small in-plane anisotropy enables almost continuous variation of the resistance via the anisotropic magnetoresistance effect. Additionally, because the lattice parameters remain very close to the critical value _γ_ crit over the entire temperature range, small strains can switch the Néel vector at any _T < T_ N, as experimentally demonstrated in Ref. [28]. Since strain is now suggested for preparing single-domain samples [29], care must be taken to account for the subtle magnetic changes it may induce. MnTe provides a rare example of a material where small strains lead to significant changes in electrically measurable properties like the AHE, unlocking new research avenues and potential technological applications. One such application is as a sensitive sensor of external stimuli: applying strain (an effective, albeit sample-dependent, tuning parameter for 

5 

the AHE [29, 30]) could produce an electrical switch-like response as the lattice parameters cross _γ_ crit. This strain can be applied directly (producing a strain sensor) or coupled to other parameters (e.g., temperature via differential thermal expansion); because this effect is intrinsic to the material, miniaturization is unlikely to be a limiting factor. Furthermore, the altermagnetic properties of MnTe (such as resilience to applied magnetic fields) and its significant magnetovolume effect [31] further broaden its potential applications. 

In summary, our _µ_ SR measurements of bulk MnTe reveal two distinct magnetically ordered regimes, explained by a change in the magnetic structure around _T[∗] ≃_ 260 K. Below _T[∗]_ , the Néel vector lies 30 _[◦]_ from the _a_ -axis, before reorienting to align with the _a_ -axis at higher _T_ . We attribute this transition to temperaturedependent single-ion anisotropies, likely driven by thermal expansion of the lattice parameters. Additional, continuously evolving modifications to the magnetic structure may be needed to fully explain the _µ_ SR spectra; the small out-of-plane tilting of the Mn moments that arises naturally from our model is a likely candidate. These discoveries highlight the extreme sensitivity of _µ_ SR to subtle magnetic changes, demonstrating its value for exploring altermagnets and other materials where precise magnetic details are critical. Because the Néel vector reorientation has easily measurable consequences for all-electrical probes (such as the AHE or anisotropic magnetoresistance), this phenomenon opens new routes for fundamental research and potential technological applications. As a room-temperature magnetically ordered material, MnTe is an exciting host for this effect, paving the way for device applications without the need for extensive materials engineering. While this phenomenon may be rare because it requires orbital quenching, it is unlikely to be unique; theoretical work should therefore seek other candidate materials with similarly appealing combinations of structural parameters and orbitally quenched magnetic moments.

## **APPENDIX: EXPERIMENTAL AND COMPUTATIONAL DETAILS** 

Single crystals of MnTe were grown via the Bridgman technique using high-purity manganese (Mn, 5N8) and tellurium (Te, 6N). Stoichiometric amounts of Mn and Te were loaded into a pointed-bottom alumina crucible and sealed under vacuum (10 _[−]_[6] mbar) in a fused-silica ampoule. The ampoule was heated in a vertical tube furnace at a rate of 1 _[◦]_ C/min to 1150 _[◦]_ C and held at this temperature for 10 hours. It was then slowly cooled to 900 _[◦]_ C at 0.03 _[◦]_ C/min, after which the furnace was switched off. The temperature gradient in the furnace was approximately 0.6 _[◦]_ C/cm. This yielded a singlecrystalline ingot approximately 8 mm in diameter and 3 cm long, from which a 1 mm-thick slice was cut. 

The _µ_ SR measurements were performed on the GPS spectrometer [32] at S _µ_ S, Paul Scherrer Institute, Switzerland. The single crystal was oriented with the _a_ - axis along the muon momentum direction (aligned with the forward-backward detectors), and the muon spin was rotated toward the _c_ -axis (aligned with the up-down detectors) for ZF measurements. In this geometry, the measurements are simultaneously sensitive to two components of the internal field. To simulate these results, muon stopping sites were calculated using the MuFinder application [33], followed by dipole field calculations with the muesr code [34].

X-ray diffraction measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab diffractometer equipped with a twobounce Ge(220) monochromator operating at the Cu _Kα_ line. The experiment was conducted in a custom-made helium cryostat (Cold Edge Technologies). To extract both the _a_ and _c_ lattice parameters, measurements were conducted in two sample orientations: (0001) and (101[¯] 0).

The MnTe2 magnetic ordering transition is clearly visible near 80 K; despite occupying a small volume fraction of the sample, the impurity yields a comparatively large magnetization. This points to a ferromagnetic response from the impurity, generating significant random stray fields that affect muons implanting in the MnTe bulk. These stray fields account for the loss of asymmetry in our _µ_ SR measurements, as they rapidly dephase the muon spin polarization and preclude reliable data collection in this temperature regime. 

Previous studies have demonstrated that the domain population, and consequently the magnetization, in MnTe is highly sensitive to both magnetic field history [S42] and relatively weak applied fields, even along the hard _c_ -axis [S43]. Consequently, interpreting bulk magnetometry data is challenging, particularly when attempting to resolve subtle phenomena such as the reorientation of the Néel vector. This further underscores the distinct advantage of local probes like _µ_ SR, which can evaluate these effects under true zero-field conditions. 

In the measurements presented in the main text, the initial asymmetry decreases at low _T_ , eventually becoming unresolvable. This indicates that the internal fields or relaxation rates, which reflect the width of the local magnetic field distribution, become too large for the time resolution of the _µ_ SR spectrometer, causing the muon polarization to dephase rapidly. The onset temperature of this signal loss correlates with the ferromagnetic ordering temperature of MnTe2 [S44], which forms inclusions throughout the bulk sample A. Furthermore, theoretical 

studies suggest that strain can shift the ordering temperature of MnTe2 by up to a factor of three [S45]; because the MnTe2 inclusions are likely strained by the surrounding MnTe matrix, the progressive loss of initial asymmetry probably arises from a gradual, spatially distributed ferromagnetic ordering of these impurity phases. 

To verify the intrinsic low- _T_ behavior of MnTe, we performed zero-field (ZF) _µ_ SR measurements on the two small, flux-grown MnTe crystals (B and C) free of MnTe2 impurities. Given their shape and size, these crystals were mounted with the _c_ -axis parallel to the beam direction, resulting in oscillations primarily observed in the forward-backward detector pair. The small sample volume introduces significant experimental challenges, notably a large background asymmetry from muons implanting in the silver degrader, sample holder, or cryostat walls. Consequently, the oscillating asymmetry in a weak transverse field does not drop to zero below _T_ N [Fig. S8(b)], and only the higher-amplitude ZF oscillation frequency is resolvable above 150 K. 

Despite these challenges, the ZF oscillation frequencies [Fig. S8(a)] closely match those of the larger crystal, confirming that sample A remains a faithful probe of the intrinsic MnTe magnetism at higher temperatures. Crucially, oscillations re-emerge in the MnTe2-free samples below 15 K, exhibiting a beating pattern in the time spectra indicative of two closely spaced high frequencies [Fig. S8(c), representing _∼_ 19 h of integrated acquisition below 10 K for sample B]. The magnitude of these internal fields is consistent with a low-temperature extrapolation of _B_ 1. This _≃_ 10% splitting of the _B_ 1 field suggests the magnetic moments cant slightly away from the _a[∗]_ axis (as supported by the muon site calculations in Fig. 4 of the main text). Such a subtle temperature dependence of the Néel vector accounts for the evolving _B_ 1 _/B_ 2 ratio below _T[∗]_ . Additionally, an anomaly in the low-temperature resistivity of similarly grown MnTe crystals [S27] further corroborates a minor structural or magnetic adjustment. Ultimately, we conclude that the fundamental magnetic structure of MnTe does not undergo significant changes below _T ≃_ 100 K, aside from this slight canting of the Néel vector.
