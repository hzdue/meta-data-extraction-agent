**Abstract:** Whether carbon forms crystalline structures beyond diamond at terapascal pressures remains unresolved. Predicted for nearly four decades, the denser BC8 phase has lacked direct crystallographic evidence. Here we report the first direct crystallographic identification of BC8 carbon, formed as a single crystal from molten carbon during laser-driven decaying-shock compression of [100]-oriented diamond following loading above 1.1 TPa. Using _in situ_ orientation-resolved single-crystal X-ray diffraction, we observe discrete Bragg spots whose spacings and angular relationships are reproduced by a common orientation matrix within each indexed BC8 reflection group, providing constraints beyond powder peak matching. Our singlecrystal formation strategy and direct diffraction evidence resolve the decades-long quest for a postdiamond carbon structure, demonstrating how dynamic loading pathways can make predicted high-pressure phases experimentally accessible.

**Main Text:** Carbon underpins core problem in condensed matter physics and planetary sciences. Its well-characterized allotropes, graphite and diamond, serve as fundamental benchmark for understanding chemical bonding and structural stability across wide pressure-temperature ranges ( _1_ – _5_ ). In the deep interiors of Uranus, Neptune, and particularly in carbon-rich exoplanets, pressures can reach the terapascal (TPa) region, alongside temperature of several thousand kelvin ( _6_ – _10_ ). Under such extreme conditions, theoretical studies spanning nearly four decades have predicted BC8 carbon, a denser tetrahedrally bonded structure that becomes thermodynamically favored over diamond at ultrahigh pressure ( _11_ – _16_ ). Independent crystal-structure searches have further identified BC8 as the stable high-pressure carbon allotrope ( _17_ ). 

Experimentally, however, BC8 carbon has remained elusive despite sustained efforts at major facilities ( _3_ , _5_ , _18_ , _19_ ) and advances in in-situ X-ray diffraction ( _4_ , _20_ – _22_ ). Ramp-compression experiments have shown that diamond can persist to pressures as high as 2 TPa without transforming to BC8 ( _3_ ), highlighting the kinetic barriers associated with the transformation. Atomistic simulations further emphasize that pressure alone is insufficient to drive subsolidus diamond-BC8 conversion and phase formation is highly sensitive to the pressure-temperature (PT) loading pathway ( _23_ – _25_ ). More recently, shock compression experiments on microcrystalline diamond reported the persistence of diamond to approximately 1 TPa and evidence of melting, again without detecting BC8 ( _26_ ). These findings suggest that accessing BC8 carbon requires more than reaching extreme pressures, highlighting the importance of the P-T history during dynamic compression. 

Our previous decaying-shock experiments on [100]-oriented single-crystal diamond established thermodynamically constraints on carbon melting and proposed BC8 formation region ( _27_ ). As the shock decayed, the inferred P-T trajectory descended from the liquid carbon regime and exhibited a sharp kink interpreted as a possible signature of BC8 crystallization. The subsequent trajectory was consistent with the proposed BC8 melting boundary to the diamond-BC8-liquid triple point near 830 GPa and 7200 K, before approaching the diamond melting boundary, and finally entered the solid-diamond phase field. This sequence was broadly consistent with the theoretically predicted phase diagram (Fig.1b). The inferred passage through the high-temperature liquid regime suggested a possible route to BC8 formation, as enhanced atomic mobility could facilitate the structural rearrangements required for crystallization. Yet whether BC8 had formed along this pathway remained unresolved. 

This high-temperature pathway also makes the crystallographic identification of BC8 particularly challenging. Although in-situ X-ray diffraction has reached terapascal pressures ( _22_ , _28_ ), rapidly evolving P-T condition can complicate diffraction measurements, while conventional polycrystalline diffraction under shock conditions may provide insufficient crystallographic information to distinguish competing high-pressure phases. Orientation-resolved SCXRD offers a more stringent approach by combining lattice-spacing information with the angular relationships among reflections, allowing candidate structures to be tested against a common crystal orientation. Here, using _in situ_ orientation-resolved SCXRD, we report the first direct crystallographic identification of BC8 carbon formed from molten carbon during laser-driven decaying-shock compression of [100]-oriented single-crystal diamond. The measured reflection spacings and angular relationships identify a single BC8 crystal lattice, extending orientation-resolved crystallographic identification to near-terapascal pressures on nanosecond timescales.

## **Results.** 

To probe carbon near the diamond-BC8-liquid phase boundaries, we combined _in-situ_ X-ray diffraction (XRD) with line-imaging VISAR at the SG-IIIp laser facility ( _21_ , _27_ , _29_ , _30_ ). A 1-ns, 351-nm laser pulse launched a decaying shock into a [100]-oriented single-crystal diamond, while a synchronized 1-ns quasi-monochromatic 8.4-keV Cu He-α X-rays pulse probed the compressed sample with diffraction recorded on image plates (Fig. 1A). 

We used the shock pressure history to identify the conditions sampled during the X-ray exposure. In shot 242, the VISAR-constrained pressure decreased from ~1.2 to 0.8 TPa during the X-ray diffraction time. Mapping this history onto the reference Hugoniot ( _5_ ) places the sampled conditions across the previously reported BC8 melting region near the triple point( _5_ , _15_ , _31_ ) (Fig.1B). Shot 366 used comparable drive and probe conditions and sampled an overlapping pressure range, allowing comparison between the two experiments. Each record therefore integrates contributions from regions experiencing spatially and temporally varying conditions. Further experimental details are provided in Materials and Methods in the supplementary materials. 

The raw diffraction records reveal numerous discrete, localized spots alongside tantalum reference features and ambient diamond reflections (Fig. 1C and Fig. S3). The representative pattern for shot 242 contains more than ten candidate reflections. Their localized appearance is consistent with diffraction from individual crystalline domains, while their radial and azimuthal positions could provide complementary constraints for lattice indexing and crystallographic orientation. 

To analyze these reflections, we mapped the image plate records onto scattering angle, 2θ, and azimuthal angle,  , using the calibrated detector geometry, with tantalum reflections providing the angular calibrations (26, 29, 30). Candidate reflections were cross-checked against the raw detector images. Using provisional BC8 Miller indices, we fitted shot-specific orthorhombic lattice metrics to twelve reflections in shot 242 and seven in shot 366. We then tested whether subsets of these reflections could be described by a common crystal orientation. For each group, a single orientation matrix was fitted using signed Miller indices, and the calculated positions were compared with observations in both 2θ and  (Fig. 2, A to D). Whereas 2θ primarily constrains interplanar spacings, the combined (2θ-  ) analysis additionally tests the relative orientation of reciprocal-lattice vectors, providing a more stringent criterion for crystallographic indexing than radial peak matching along (see Materials and Methods in supplementary materials). 

In shot 242, three highlighted reflections, including (2[̅] 00), (112) and (130), were approximately compatible with a common crystal orientation, yielding an angular root-mean-square (RMS) deviation of 1.64° (Fig. 2, A and B). We retained this association as a candidate group, while treating additional reflection pairs as tentative assignments. In shot 366, the three highlighted reflections, including (002), (12̅   1̅)and (22̅   0), were reproduced by a common orientation matrix with a smaller angular RMS mismatch of 0.48° (Fig. 2, C and D). With the fitted lattice metrics fixed, leave-one-out tests using each pair of reflections predict the position of the omitted third reflection within the nominal 2° criterion. One additional reflection pair remains conditional. These results provide stronger orientation-based support for a common crystalline domain in shot 366. 

We next examined the lattice-spacing relationships characteristic of BC8 (Fig. 3, A and B). In the ideal cubic BC8, symmetry-equivalent reflections such as (200), (020) and (002) have identical interplanar spacings. The orthorhombic metrics used in our analysis allow these spacings to split, while retaining reflections-family relationships characteristic of the BC8 framework. Assuming 16 carbon atoms per conventional BC8 unit cell, the fitted volumes yield nearly identical densities of 7.19 ± 0.45 g cm⁻³ for shot 242 and 7.20 ± 0.46 g cm⁻³ for shot 366, with uncertainties reported at ±2σ. An equal-weight combination of the two shots distribution gives a representative density of 7.19 ± 0.45 g cm⁻³ (Fig. 3C). Guided by the previous melting study (25), we consider 830–1000 GPa as the inferred pressure window for BC8 crystallization during shock decay (Fig. 3D). Within this window, the measured density exceeds the liquid reference trends as expected for a denser solid coexisting with liquid. Assuming a positive entropy of fusion, this density ordering is also consistent with a positive BC8 melting-curve slope through the Clapeyron relation. As a further comparison, a density trend anchored at the previously inferred BC8 density of 6.95 g cm⁻³ at 830 GPa and following the mean slope of the liquid reference trends reaches approximately 7.10 g cm⁻³ at 915 GPa. This value lies within the experimental uncertainty, supporting consistency between the diffraction-derived density and the previously inferred the thermodynamic constraints. 

## **Discussion.** 

Taken together, our results establish the first direct crystallographic identification of BC8 carbon. The crystallographic assignment is supported by reflections forbidden for ideal diamond, while the agreement between lattice-derived densities from two independent experiments provides an additional consistency check. Departures from ideal cubic spacings are consistent with lattice distortion at near-terapascal pressures and temperatures of several thousand kelvin. Spatial and temporal variations in pressure, temperature and stress may contribute to these deviations, although their microscopic origin remains unresolved. Further diffraction measurements with improved temporal and spatial resolution, potentially enabled by coupling high-energy laser drivers with ultrafast X-ray free-electron laser probes ( _4_ , _20_ ), could help clarify the origin of these distortions. 

The formation of BC8 carbon from molten carbon establishes a high-temperature route to this long-predicted post-diamond phase, highlighting the importance of thermodynamic pathways in accessing new crystalline structures under extreme compression. This discovery provides an experimental crystallographic benchmark for models of dense carbon relevant to planetary interiors and high-density carbon ablators in inertial confinement fusion. More broadly, this advance in extreme-condition crystallography opens new opportunities for identifying highpressure crystalline phases in other materials. Future recovery experiments could determine whether BC8 carbon can be retained upon decompression ( _32_ ), paving the way for investigating the predicted superhard and semiconducting properties of this long-sought carbon allotrope.
