The high-pressure behavior of silicon telluride (Si2Te3), a two-dimensional (2D) layered material, was investigated using synchrotron X-ray powder diffraction in a diamond anvil cell to 11 _._ 5 GPa coupled with first-principles theory. Si2Te3 undergoes a phase transition at _<_ 1 GPa from a trigonal to a hexagonal crystal structure. At higher pressures ( _>_ 8 _._ 5 GPa), X-ray diffraction showed the appearance of new peaks possibly coincident with a new phase transition, though we suspect Si2Te3 retains a hexagonal structure. Density functional theory calculations of the band structure reveal metallization above 9 _._ 1 GPa consistent with previous measurements of the Raman spectra and disappearance of color and transparency at pressure. The theoretical Raman spectra reproduce the prominent features of the experiment, though a deeper analysis suggests that the orientation of Si dimers dramatically influences the vibrational response. Given the complex structure of Si2Te3, simulation of the resulting high-pressure phase is complicated by disordered vacancies and the initial orientations of Si–Si dimers in the crushed layered phase. 

## **I. INTRODUCTION** 

Silicon telluride (Si2Te3) is a brilliant red, transparent, two-dimensional (2D) layered material that offers promise for its optoelectronic properties [1–4], intercalation capabilities [1, 5–7], and because it is uniquely compatible with current silicon semiconductor processing technologies. Si2Te3 exhibits photoluminescence from defect trap states around 650 nm offering promise as a photomaterial [2, 8]. Measurements show possible evidence of memristor behavior [6], and recent calculations suggest that Si2Te3 is a superior thermoelectric with an unprecedented ZT of 1.86 [9]. 

From a fundamental perspective, Si2Te3 shows a unique thermodynamic phase behavior. Johnson _et al._ [10] demonstrated that Si2Te3 likely undergoes a possible semiconductor-to-metal electronic phase transition under pressure at 9 _._ 5 _±_ 0 _._ 5 GPa using Raman scattering of single plates in a diamond anvil cell. Optical measurements showed that silicon telluride went from transparent red to opaque grey/black under pressure. Further, Raman modes vanished at high pressures suggesting a change in the polarizability of the crystal. However, these studies lacked structural investigations to identify any accompanying phase transitions. Despite a wealth of novel properties, the fundamental thermodynamic phase behavior of silicon telluride remains largely unknown. Calculations by Bhattarai and Shen [11] suggested that compression beyond 7 GPa induces a pressure-driven phase transition from a semiconductor to a metal with a coincident structural change. Other calculations also suggest that silicon telluride compressed in a silicon-based pressure medium could lead to the formation of SiTe [12], a long-sought phase that is expected to exhibit topological 

insulator properties and is impossible to achieve under ambient pressures [13, 14]. The change in stoichiometry, though, seems unsupported by experiment. Grzechnik _et al._ [15] investigated pressure-dependent phases of silicon telluride by mixing powders of Si and Te and heating them at high pressures and temperatures in a large volume multi-anvil press while performing _in situ_ synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD). Several unique phases that can be formed from mixtures of silicon and tellurium were identified [15]. They also measured bulk synthesized Si2Te3 as a function of pressure and temperature. At elevated temperatures, Grzechnik _et al._ [15] found several different phases from silicon and tellurium including a Mn5Si3 hexagonal type phase and several clathrates. In the same article, bulk-grown Si2Te3 was compressed at ambient temperature and it was found that above 12 GPa, Si2Te3 undergoes a phase change to amorphous; however, no data of these measurements was provided to adequately evaluate these conclusions. 

In this joint experimental and computational investigation, we use a diamond anvil cell to hydrostatically compress vapor-phase grown Si2Te3 nanoplates to high pressures at ambient temperature. _In situ_ synchroton X- ray diffraction at pressure reveals that Si2Te3 undergoes a phase transition at fairly low pressures ( _∼_ 0 _._ 2 GPa), corresponding to a structural change from trigonal ( _P_ 31 _c_ ) to hexagonal with a higher symmetry ( _P_ 6 _/mmm_ ). Peaks associated with the van der Waals (vdW) gap vanish as expected in layered materials [16]. At higher pressures ( _>_ 8 _._ 5 GPa), X-ray diffraction shows new peaks that cannot be indexed with any certainty as a hexagonal crystal. No amorphization is seen to 11 _._ 5 GPa, and returning to ambient pressures does not restore the original structure. _In-situ_ XRD and Raman spectroscopy measurements are corroborated with density functional theory (DFT) calculations, identifying a semiconductorto-metal phase transition in Si2Te3 [10]. Our calculations reveal a structural phase transition that eliminates

## **II. METHODS** 

## **A. Experimental** 

## _1. Synthesis_ 

Silicon telluride was synthesized using previously developed vapor-phase methods [1]. Briefly, a ceramic boat containing a mixture of 0 _._ 18 g Te and 0 _._ 07 g Si was placed in the center of a 25 _._ 4 mm quartz tube. Upstream, a ceramic boat containing 0 _._ 10 g Te was placed. Downstream, at 12 cm–15 cm from the center, a quartz substrate was used for the large area vapor-liquid-solid (VLS) growth. The furnace was heated to 800 _[◦]_ C at a ramp rate of 60 _[◦]_ C per minute. Once the furnace reached 800 _[◦]_ C, the furnace was slowly cooled to room temperature. Plates were stored in separate containers under inert N2 in a glovebox. 

## _2. Characterization_ 

Powder X-ray diffraction of Si2Te3 at ambient conditions was collected on a Bruker Eco Advance with a Cu K _α_ source ( _λ_ = 1 _._ 54[˚] A). TEM images and electron diffraction (SAED) were collected on a JEOL 2100fac. Raman spectra were acquired on a home-built system with an inverted Leica DMi8, Princeton Instruments Isoplane SC320 with an 1800 groove/mm grating, Pixis CCD Camera, and Coherent Sapphire SF laser at _λ_ = 532 _._ 15 nm at _∼_ 5 mW laser power. A Semrock RazorEdge filter and dichroic give a cutoff of _∼_ 70–100 cm _[−]_[1] . Acquisition times were 5 seconds with 5 spectral averages. Ultraviolet (UPS) was collected on the Kratos Axis Supra using a He-I (21 _._ 22 eV) source with an applied bias of _−_ 9 V. Optical images were acquired on a 

Leica M205C. Characterization of Si2Te3 including optical images, X-ray photoelectron spectra, and scanning electron microscopy energy dispersive characterization of the elemental constituency can be found in Fig. S1 in the supporting information (SI). 

## _3. High-Pressure X-ray Diffraction Measurements_ 

A gas membrane-driven symmetric diamond anvil cell from Deutsches Elektronen-Synchrotron (DESY) with 400 µm diamond culets was used for high-pressure measurements. Spring-steel gaskets were pre-indented to _∼_ 100 µm thickness and a centered 200 µm diameter gasket hole was drilled with a Cameron Micro Drill Press Series 214-D3. Several grains of annealed ruby spheres from Alamax were placed into the gasket hole to measure the pressure inside the diamond cell. Ethylcyclohexane (ECH) was used as a pressure-transmitting medium. Loading the sample was done using an acupuncture needle to scrape the Si2Te3 growth substrate and place the platelets into the DAC. A drop of ECH was placed onto the substrate; the substrate was scratched with the needle and then drawn back up into the syringe and dropped into the DAC which was quickly closed. The sample loading is done in less than a minute from exposure of the Si2Te3 to complete loading to prevent oxidation of the Si2Te3. ECH is not a very hydrostatic pressure medium but other media either oxidize the sample, like 4 : 1 methanol ethanol or silicone oil or are not as easy to load with Si2Te3, like 1 : 1 pentane : isopentane. Ethylcyclohexane has been used in several diamond anvil cell investigations previously and maintains somewhat hydrostatic conditions to about 4 GPa [17]. 

High-pressure X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD) were acquired on high-pressure beamline 12.2.2 of the Advanced Light Source using an energy of _∼_ 25 keV ( _λ_ = 0 _._ 496[˚] A) [18]. XRD was acquired using a Pilatus 2 M detector. Typical acquisition times were between 2–10 minutes. The sample to detector distance and detector tilt were calibrated with a CeO2 NIST standard. All 2D XRD images were integrated into 1D patterns using Dioptas [19]. The pressure was determined using the ruby fluorescence method. About 2–3 ruby spheres were placed into the cell and used to determine the pressure. Ruby fluorescence was acquired using an online ruby system with a 488 nm laser to determine the pressure [20, 21]. 

XRD pattern indexing was performed using the DICVOL program [22] and Treor90 [23, 24] implemented in FULLPROF [25] through MATCH [26]. Structure solutions were also determined using GSAS-II [27]. Lattice parameters were confirmed via LeBail analysis using FOX (Free Objects for Crystallography) [28]. ## **B. Theoretical** 

## _1. Computational Details_ 

First-principles electronic structure DFT calculations are performed using Quantum ESPRESSO (QE) v7.2 [29–31]. To appropriately model layered Si2Te3, we choose an exchange-correlation functional that contains an additional nonlocal term to describe van der Waals interactions (vdW-DF-cx)[32–36]. The Si2Te3 crystal geometry is obtained from Ploog _et al._ [37] and was used to conduct our DFT simulations. Atomic sites are described by projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials from the PSLibrary [38]. Plane waves are expanded up to a kinetic energy cutoff of 60 Ry (240 Ry) for wavefunctions (charge density), and the self-consistent field (SCF) convergence threshold is set to 10 _[−]_[8] Ry. A Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack mesh is sampled with a resolution of 6 _×_ 6 _×_ 6. Electronic densities of states (DOS) are computed from a non-SCF diagonalization on a 12 _×_ 12 _×_ 12 k-points grid centered at the Γ-point with occupations calculated with the tetrahedron method [39]. 

tion, kinetic energy cutoffs are increased to 80 Ry and 320 Ry for the wavefunctions and charge density, which achieves an estimated accuracy of 10 _[−]_[5] Ry for total energy. 

LDA gives similar structural and vibrational properties as the vdW-DF-cx functional for other layered chalcogenides, e.g., MoS2[42]. The structures previously obtained with PAW pseudo potentials are optimized to equilibrium with NC pseudo potentials by relaxing atomic positions until all forces converge below 10 _[−]_[5] . Structural reorganization is minor and the resulting atomic displacements do not exceed 0 _._ 1[˚] A. Nonresonant Raman coefficients and macroscopic dielectric constants are computed at the Γ point. SCF energy is converged to 10 _[−]_[8] Ry, and the acoustic sum rule is imposed on the dynamical matrix together with nonanalytical corrections to facilitate LO–TO splitting near Γ. Using the calculated interatomic force constants and phonon frequencies, phonon polarizations are obtained as the eigenvectors of the dynamical matrix and visualized with the Interactive Phonon Visualizer [43].

## **B. Theoretical** 

_1. Layered Phase Structure_ 

The initial structure of Si2Te3 was determined from prior XRD experiments [1, 37, 46]. At ambient pressures, Si2Te3 has a trigonal crystal structure with 12 Te atoms and 8 Si atoms in the conventional cell. The Te atoms are arranged in a hexagonal geometry, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Si2Te3 possesses a relatively complicated structure because pairs of Si atoms form dimers (dumbbells) that partially occupy various positions and orientations between the Te layers. Within each Te layer reside two unique site clusters that host one silicon dimer with probabilistic orientation, which results in a disordered crystal. Two of the dumbbells have four possible orientations as shown in Fig. 3(b), and their _measured_ occupation factors are as follows: The first is an out-of-plane (vertical) orientation with 47% occupation; the other three possible orientations are in-plane (horizontal), each occupied with equal probability of 18%. There is no out-of-plane orientation for the Si dumbbell for the Si clusters that are not vertically aligned between layers. Instead, there are only three in-plane orientations, each with an equal probability of 33% [37, 47]. 

Partial occupancy among the sites forming silicon dimers presents a unique challenge for our theoretical analysis of Si2Te3. In the unit cell, there are 1001 possible configurations. To tackle the complexity of partial occupation, we used a site permutation search (SPS) algorithm in a unit cell [48] that allows us to identify the Si2Te3 structure with the minimal energy. Due to the relatively small number of possible configurations in a unit cell, the SPS algorithm is used to predict the formation energy of every configuration, utilizing the MEGNet pretrained Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (CGCNN), and identify the structure with the lowest energy [49]. Finally, the geometry was optimized by relaxing both cell parameters and atomic positions at 0 GPa in QE. The resulting structure is hereafter referred to as 

5 

the “layered phase”. Slight distortions from a hexagonal structure were incurred by freely optimizing the cell parameters. The angle between the basal lattice vectors **a** and **b** increases to 122 _._ 1 _[◦]_ , and the angle between **b** and **c** decreases to 89 _._ 4 _[◦]_ . We attribute this minor divergence from the hexagonal structure to our choice of a single orientation for each of the silicon dimers, which does not accurately capture the partial occupation observed experimentally.

## _2. Layered Phase of Si2Te3 at Ambient Conditions_ 

The Si2Te3 ground state layered phase at 0 GPa is shown in Fig. 4(a). This configuration has 50 % of the Si dumbbells aligned vertically and the other 50 % aligned in the plane. In this case, both layers in the unit cell have exactly one pair of vertical Si dumbbells. The layered phase unit contains 24 Si–Te bonds and 4 Si–Si bonds with respective lengths of 2 _._ 55 A and 2[˚] _._ 31 A.[˚] Each Si site is tetrahedrally coordinated, forming bonds with three Te atoms and one other Si atom, while each Te atom bonds with two Si atoms. The mean value of Si–Si–Te (Te–Si–Te) bond angles is 106 _._ 0 _±_ 3 _._ 7 _[◦]_ (112 _._ 00 _±_ 5 _._ 88 _[◦]_ ). 

The layered phase is a semiconductor with an indirect band gap of 1 _._ 01 eV, as shown in Fig. S4(a) and Fig. 7(c), which is very close to the experimental indirect band gap of 0 _._ 98 eV, measured at ambient conditions [2]. The direct band gap is not accurately captured by DFT. Other studies have employed the GW and Bethe-Salpeter Equation (BSE) methods to refine predictions of the direct band gap in layered Si2Te3 [50, 51]. The calculated density of states (DOS) for the layered phase agrees well with the experimental ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) spectrum as shown in Fig. 5(b), where the Fermi energy is aligned to 0 eV and the highest peaks of the DOS and UPS spectrum are scaled to the same magnitude. 

Although our DFT calculations of the layered phase accurately predict the electronic properties, the experimental phononic properties cannot be fully reproduced by a single unit-cell structure. In fact, the different orientations of the Si dimers contribute uniquely to the Raman spectrum of Si2Te3 [51], which we demonstrate by constructing two additional models with varying distributions of the vertical Si dumbbells. i. a layered phase with a vertical Si dumbbell in each layer, with 50 % of all Si dumbbells oriented vertically; ii. a phase where a vertical Si dumbbell in one layer is flipped to the horizontal orientation, referred to as the 25 % phase (shown in Fig. S5(a)); iii. a phase with no vertical Si dumbbells, such that all vertical Si dumbbells are flipped into the in-plane orientations, referred to as the 0% vertical phase (shown in Fig. S5(b)).

## _4. Compression above 7 GPa and Pressure Release_ 

Before any structural phase transition occurs, Si2Te3 compresses more readily in the direction normal to the vdW layers (axis- _c_ ). To promote a merger between layers, we simulate static compression by contracting _c/a_ (the length ratio between axis- _c_ and axis- _a_ ). This pro- 

cess induces the formation of bonds between layers and eventually a bulk structure. The energy change in the process of the static compression is described in Fig. 6(a). A movie showing the structural change during the compression is provided in the SI. Atomic positions are fully relaxed at each compression step, and the total energy is carefully monitored to identify steps that undergo significant structural rearrangement. When reducing _c/a_ from 1.85 to 1.2, Si2Te3 undergoes a two-step phase transition. During the static compression, while _c/a_ remains larger than 1.35, the structure remains in the layered phase and the distance between layers is reduced. Reducing _c/a_ below 1.35 results in the formation of interlayer covalent bonds (shown in Fig. 6(a)), accompanied by a reorganization of the atomic geometry that reduces the pressure along axis _c_ from 11 _._ 4 GPa to 8 _._ 9 GPa and the total SCF energy by 11 meV _/_ atom. This structure, which we call the intermediate phase, is maintained as _c/a_ and is further reduced to 1.28, achieving a pressure of 10 GPa. When _c/a_ reaches 1.27, corresponding to a pressure of 10 _._ 8 GPa, the vdW stacks are entirely converted into a bulk phase, and the atomic geometry rearranges dramatically. The pairs of horizontal Si dumbbells rotate completely into the x-y plane, forming bonds horizontally with other Si dumbbells, and vertically aligned dumbbells connect between layers. As shown in Fig. 6(a), this structural transition at _c/a_ = 1 _._ 27 reduces the total SCF energy by 31 meV _/_ atom. We select the structure with _c/a_ = 1 _._ 25, which has a pressure of 14 GPa but is not at hydrostatic conditions. Cell parameters are optimized to a hydrostatic pressure of 14 GPa. We refer to the resulting bulk phase, shown in Fig. 4(b), as the high-pressure phase. 

Decompression of the sample is simulated by incrementally scaling the lattice vectors by a constant factor. The principle cell vector (axis- _a_ ) is enlarged from 7 _._ 4[˚] A to 8 _._ 5 A[˚] in 40 steps, while axis- _b_ and axis- _c_ are scaled with the same proportions. Between each step atomic positions are relaxed to equilibrium, followed by the cell parameters under the pressure of the previous calculation. The structure that achieves the lowest total energy is relaxed at 0 GPa and hereafter referred to as the released phase. 

The band structure and density of states for the highpressure phase are shown in Fig. S4(b) and Fig. 7(c). There is no band gap, indicating that the high-pressure phase is metallic. This agrees with experiments, where no Raman response is detected (Fig. 7(a)), and the optical signature changes from transparent red to opaque grey/black under pressure. The structure of the highpressure phase is shown in Fig. 4(b). There are 20 Si– Te bonds and 8 Si–Si bonds in a unit cell. Compared with the layered phase, 4 Si–Te bonds break, and 4 Si–Si bonds form during the static compression. There are 12 Te atoms in the unit cell, 4 of which bond with 1 Si atom while the other 8 bond with 2 Si atoms. Si atoms always have between 3 and 6 neighbors. ## _5. Model Validity and the Influence of Occupational Disorder_ 

Although the theoretical treatment of only three phases cannot capture the properties of occupational disorder, the electronic and phononic properties remain similar to experimental values, both qualitatively and quantitatively. Numerous theoretical treatments of the Si2Te3 system in the past decade [9, 12, 46, 47, 51, 53–55] present different structures, but still agree on several calculated properties. In our study, the density of states for the three arrangements of Si dumbbells at ambient conditions (Fig. 5(b)) do not have significant qualitative differences. The calculated indirect band gaps for the layered phase, 25% vertical phase and 0% vertical phase are 1 _._ 01 eV, 0 _._ 88 eV, and 1 _._ 01 eV, respectively. All are within the uncertainty of past UV–Vis absorption experiments that report an indirect band gap of about 1 eV [2]. A detailed discussion of the experimental analysis is presented in the supporting information. 

Two mysteries remain in the observed properties of Si2Te3. The first is the mechanism of the defect trap states that can be populated by illuminating the sample with band gap energy photons [2, 8, 51], which we attribute to a spectrum of closely spaced momentumindirect exciton states originating from dimer disorder and exhibiting phonon-assisted photoluminescence [56]. Second, the energetic ground state configuration of the layered phase requires 50 % of the Si dimers to orient vertically, while XRD measurements indicate that only 23 _._ 5 % Si dimers are vertically aligned [1, 37]. VLS synthesis techniques cause precipitation of nanoplates or nanocrystals from a droplet of liquid Te and Si [1]. Si dimer orientations are governed by kinetics at the liquidsolid interface during synthesis and become thermody- 

namically locked as the layers grow. In our model, the energy difference between the 50% vertical and 0% vertical configurations is 12 _._ 5 meV _/_ atom, indicating that the energy difference between the two orientations is less than the equipartition kinetic energy at ambient conditions. Transitioning a Si dimer from one orientation to another after synthesis, however, would require the redistribution of covalent electrons and a relatively high energy barrier. Shen _et al._ [47] find from _ab initio_ nudged elastic band (NEB) calculations that dimer orientations are separated by an energy barrier of approximately 1 eV, preventing dimers from changing configuration at the ambient temperature. 

## **IV. CONCLUSION** 

This work elucidates the behavior of Si2Te3 under high pressure through first-principles calculations and experiments. A low-pressure phase transition ( _<_ 1 GPa) is found coincident with a trigonal to hexagonal change in the crystal structure. The hexagonal structure is maintained up to 8 GPa. No amorphization is seen up to 11 _._ 5 GPa in these experiments, though new peaks are found in the XRD above 8 _._ 5 GPa. First-principles calculations capture the metallization of silicon telluride at high pressure (above 9 _._ 1 GPa) as seen in previous Raman experiments of Johnson _et al._ [10], and characteristic Raman signatures are attributed to the orientation of Si dimers using DFPT calculations. Because Raman modes are sensitive to the Si dimer orientation, we construct a three-phase model to explain the experimental Raman spectra at lower pressure. At high pressure, the Raman spectrum agrees with our experimental results due to metallization and is not dramatically influenced by the initial dimer orientation. The most complicated, and unresolved, task is to explain the structural properties of the high-pressure phase in terms of the experimental XRD since partial occupancy and finite size effects strongly influence the resulting geometry. There is no evident change in Si2Te3 stoichiometry with pressure, contrary to previous theoretical studies [12, 15]. Our investigation reveals interesting trends in both electronic and vibrational properties of Si2Te3 under hydrostatic compression and includes an accurate theoretical description of the structural phase transition observed experimentally at 9 _._ 1 GPa.
