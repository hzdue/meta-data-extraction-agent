## **Abstract** 

Spatial heterogeneity in lithiation complicates the interpretation of electrodeaveraged electrochemical measurements and the assessment of local active-material utilization. Here, we use three-energy X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) nanotomography to investigate lithiation distributions in NMC532 composite electrodes with active-material loadings of 3 and 15 mg cm _[−]_[2] . Tomographic acquisitions at 8.30, 8.35, and 8.43 keV combine three-dimensional particle morphology with Ni K-edge chemical sensitivity at a processed voxel size of 210 nm. The normalized absorption contrast serves as a relative lithiation indicator, without assigning absolute lithium concentrations. Measurements were performed _in situ_ after 1 h of open-circuit relaxation at selected electrochemical states. In the lowerloading electrode, four successive states during interrupted discharge at 2 mA cm _[−]_[2] reveal an increasing lithium-rich volume fraction alongside core–shell-like distributions, nonuniform peripheral enrichment, and particle-to-particle variability. In the higher-loading electrode, two regions examined at 50% depth of discharge exhibit different lithiation-sensitive contrast distributions despite sharing the same nominal electrochemical state. These observations document heterogeneity within particles, between particles, and between electrode regions, showing how overall lithiation progression can coexist with spatially nonuniform local states. Three-energy XANES nanotomography thus provides a means of examining lithiation heterogeneity in dense composite electrodes with limited spectral sampling. Correlative phasecontrast imaging could further relate these distributions to the arrangement of the carbon–binder domain and pore network. 

_**Keywords—**_ NMC532 cathodes; three-energy XANES nanotomography; in situ chemical imaging; multiscale lithiation heterogeneity; three-dimensional electrode characterization; highloading composite electrodes

## **1 Introduction** 

Lithium-ion batteries underpin the electrification of transport and the deployment of electrochemical energy storage, which keeps pressure on energy density, power capability, and durability [1, 2, 3, 4]. Reaching these targets depends not only on active material chemistry but also on the architecture of composite electrodes. Porous electrodes offer a large interfacial area for charge transfer while hosting pathways for ionic and electronic transport. Their performance therefore emerges from the coupled behavior of active particles, the electrolyte-filled pore network, and the carbon–binder domain (CBD). Densifying electrodes to raise their volumetric energy density makes uniform access to these pathways harder to maintain [5]. Spatial variations in phase distribution and connectivity can then generate heterogeneous reaction rates, uneven active material utilization, and localized degradation [6, 7, 8]. 

The CBD embodies the competing transport requirements of composite electrode design. Conductive additives promote electronic connectivity, yet their distribution can also alter pore accessibility, ionic tortuosity, and the fraction of particle surface open to electrochemical exchange [9, 10]. Forouzan _et al._ measured local electronic conductivity to relate electrode microstructure to transport properties [11]. Such approaches are informative. Translating structural heterogeneity into spatial variations in lithiation, however, requires complementary measurements of the local chemical state. Resolving these variations within particles and across electrode regions is decisive for separating particle-scale behavior from the collective response of the composite electrode. 

Raman microscopy [12], X-ray diffraction (XRD) [13, 14], X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) [15], and spatially resolved X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) measurements [16, 17, 18] track structural transformations and redox evolution from complementary angles. Conventional ensemble measurements, however, average over heterogeneous populations, and two-dimensional transmission images integrate contributions along the beam direction. Without depth-resolved information, overlapping particles and internal chemical gradients are hard to tell apart. Threedimensional imaging eases this ambiguity: it separates spatial contributions and ties local chemical states to the surrounding morphology [19, 20]. 

X-ray tomography is now an established tool for characterizing electrode architecture, from particle morphology and pore connectivity to structural changes during electrochemical cycling [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Coupled with electrochemical measurements and complementary contrast mechanisms, it extends from structure toward the spatially resolved study of electrode reactions. Finegan _et al._ demonstrated operando XRD computed tomography to resolve crystallographic heterogeneity within and between lithium manganese oxide particles [28]. More recently, Vanpeene _et al._ benchmarked X-ray nanotomography workflows across six battery materials and stressed the weight of acquisition conditions, image quality, and representative sampling in reliable morphological quantification [29]. Spatial resolution must thus be weighed together with measurement fidelity and the representativeness of the analyzed volume. 

Adding XANES to transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) brings sensitivity to local oxidation state and chemical environment. Meirer _et al._ established three-dimensional chemical imaging of the NiO-to-Ni conversion reaction, showing how spectroscopic tomography exposes chemical heterogeneity within complex electrode structures [30]. Later studies carried spatially resolved XANES over to intercalation materials. Tan _et al._ investigated nanoscale state-of-charge heterogeneity in polycrystalline nickel-rich cathodes, and Kimura _et al._ used CT-XANES to visualize three-dimensional reaction distributions in a composite solid-state battery electrode [31, 32]. Uematsu _et al._ recently combined two-dimensional TXM-XAFS (X-ray Absorption Fine-structure Spectroscopy) measurements, three-dimensional chemical imaging, and clustering analysis to examine heterogeneous charging and its relation to particle position within a positive electrode [33]. Together, these studies establish chemical imaging as a bridge between local redox behavior and electrode organization. 

Extending three-dimensional chemical imaging to dense composite electrodes is bound by a three-way trade-off between spatial resolution, chemical sensitivity, and acquisition time. Studies of individual particles, and of electrode configurations optimized for X-ray imaging, have yielded valuable insight into local reaction behavior; dense electrodes add constraints of their own, from X-ray attenuation to tomographic access [16, 34, 35]. In XANES tomography, each additional energy lengthens the measurement and adds cumulative exposure, which can perturb the very electrochemical behavior under study [36, 37]. These constraints call for reduced-energy acquisition strategies that keep sensitivity to lithiation heterogeneity while limiting the number of tomographic scans. 

Here, we pair TXM nanotomography with a three-energy acquisition scheme at the Ni K-edge to investigate lithiation heterogeneity in LiNi0 _._ 5Mn0 _._ 3Co0 _._ 2O2 composite electrodes. Measurements

They resolve lithiation distributions within secondary particles and across electrode regions. The approach tackles a practical problem, obtaining spatially resolved chemical contrast in dense electrodes from limited spectral sampling, and provides a basis for investigating persistent lithiation heterogeneity in relation to electrode microstructure. 

## **2 Experimental Section** 

## **2.1 Electrode composition and loading** 

The two positive electrodes investigated share one composition: a mixture of LiNi0.5Mn0.3Co0.2O2 (NMC) as AM, conductive carbon black and a mixture of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVdF) (96%w AM, 2%w carbon black and 2%w PVdF). They differ in AM loading. MX-A, made in our laboratory, has a very low loading ( _c.a._ 3 mg/cm²) and 30% porosity after calendaring. MX-B is a commercial electrode with a loading of 15 mg/cm² and 28% porosity after calendaring. 

## **2.2 Sample preparation for nanotomography** 

Our setup requires X-rays to cross at least the full thickness of the electrode, and the path length grows as the rotation angle sweeps through [-150°,150°] (assuming that the glassy carbon windows, the Celgard 2500 and the Li foil are mostly transparent). As a transmission technique, it needs enough X-rays leaving the sample to give sufficient signal on the detector. Of the two electrodes used in this work, MX-B is the problem: it is considerably thicker. Without preparation, its rotation angle is limited to [-75°, 75°]. Sample preparation is therefore decisive for MX-B, both for result quality and for limiting the dead angle during acquisition. 

Preparation starts from a “free-standing” electrode, obtained by simply peeling the porous electrode material off the current collector foil. A ZEISS laser dissection then cuts a precise pattern from the bulk electrode (see Figure 1d). The pattern, together with the void around it, shortens the X-ray path over a wide rotation range [-120°, 120°], reducing the dead angle from 75° ° to 30 . Smaller dead angles make the thicker MX-B usable for the experiment. To spare the region of interest from laser damage, the laser was operated in pulse mode rather than permanent mode. 

## **2.3 Electrochemical cell configurations and assembly** 

Acquiring these datasets demands sufficient X-ray transmission and angular access through the electrochemical cell. Figures 1b, c, and e present the imaging setup and cell configurations. The custom cell comprises a stainless-steel cover, a movable stainless-steel disc, and a Teflon base; its geometry limits angular obstruction during tomography while preserving electrochemical function. The design has been described in detail previously.[38] 

Cell geometry also caps the accessible electrode thickness, since it sets both the X-ray path length and the angular range available for tomography. Two cell configurations were therefore used for the _in situ_ measurements. The AMPIX cell, developed at Argonne National Laboratory for synchrotron scattering and spectroscopy experiments,[39, 40] served for the thinner MX-A electrode. It supports electrochemical measurements, but its thickness and narrow angular aperture make it ill-suited to TXM tomography, increasingly so as electrode thickness grows. Its use was consequently restricted to MX-A, to keep the combined transmission and angular-access constraints in check. 

For the higher-loading MX-B electrode (15 mg cm _[−]_[2] ), the custom cell described above offered a geometry better suited to tomographic acquisition. Its reduced thickness and wider angular aperture were designed to improve X-ray access while maintaining electrical contact and compression of the electrode stack. Assembly follows coin-cell principles, adapted to sample rotation and transmission imaging. This configuration enabled the thicker electrode to be studied under the electrochemical conditions described in the Experimental Section. 

The _in situ_ cell was assembled in an argon-filled glovebox. The electrode was placed at the center of the cell, just above the glassy carbon window. For MX-B, keeping the laser-cut patterns near the center of the windows makes them easier to locate during acquisition. Lithium foil served as the counter electrode, 1.2 M LiPF6 (dissolved in a solvent of 50% EC and 50% DMC by weight) as the electrolyte, and Celgard 2500 as the separator. The cell was designed to minimize the dead 

3 

angle during acquisition. Cells were verified to cycle stably under constant current. The same design is compatible with other _in situ_ experiments, such as X-ray diffraction in transmission. 

## **2.4 Cycling protocol and interrupted-state measurements** 

Electrochemical cycling was performed with a BioLogic multipotentiostat (SP200) within a voltage window of 2.5-4.3 V versus Li/Li[+] . Both electrodes were first charged galvanostatically at C/10 to 4.3 V, then held potentiostatically at 4.3 V for 60 min. 

Discharge followed at an areal current density of 2 mA cm _[−]_[2] for MX-A and at a nominal rate of 1C for MX-B, both to a lower cutoff voltage of 2.5 V. MX-B discharge also included a potentiostatic hold at 2.5 V. For comparison, a reference specific capacity of 180 mAh g _[−]_[1] gives nominal areal capacities of approximately 0.54 and 2.70 mAh cm _[−]_[2] for MX-A and MX-B, respectively. Under this assumption, 2 mA cm _[−]_[2] corresponds to approximately 3.7C for MX-A. 

At selected discharge states, the current was interrupted and the cells relaxed at open circuit for 1 h before tomographic acquisition. The three-energy datasets were then collected at zero current for both electrode configurations. The maps therefore capture lithiation after relaxation, not instantaneous distributions during galvanostatic operation. 

## **2.5 Three-energy XANES tomographic acquisition** 

X-ray imaging was performed on the transmission X-ray microscope at beamline 32-ID-C of the Advanced Photon Source (APS). Tomographic reconstruction used the total-variation (TV) algorithm implemented in _TomoPy_ . Reference Ni K-edge XANES spectra were acquired for the pristine electrode and for an electrode charged to 4.3 V versus Li/Li[+] , over 8.30–8.43 keV in 150 energy steps. These spectra set the three acquisition energies: 8.30 keV below the edge, 8.35 keV in the near-edge region, and 8.43 keV above the edge (Figure 1). At each selected electrochemical state, after the relaxation period described above, three tomographic datasets were acquired in sequence, one per energy, with the cell at open circuit. Each dataset comprised 721 projections over an angular range of _−_ 120 _[◦]_ to +120 _[◦]_ , with a field of view of approximately 50 _×_ 50 _µ_ m[2] . Each three-dimensional tomographic dataset required approximately 3 min of acquisition 

Restricting acquisition to three energies cuts the number of tomographic scans relative to more densely sampled XANES measurements, such as the 12-energy approach of Nowack _et al._ [16] Under otherwise identical conditions, acquisition time and cumulative X-ray exposure fall accordingly. The price is spectral: each voxel carries lithiation-sensitive absorption contrast, not a complete XANES spectrum. 

## **2.6 Volume processing and inter-energy registration** 

The reconstructed volumes had a native voxel size of approximately 30 nm. To reduce noise and facilitate inter-energy registration, they were binned using 7 _×_ 7 _×_ 7 voxel blocks, yielding a processed voxel size of 210 nm. This choice was informed by Kimura _et al._ [32], who reported limited additional noise reduction beyond 6 _×_ 6 _×_ 6 voxel binning. The resulting voxel size defines the spatial sampling interval rather than the effective chemical imaging resolution, which also depends on reconstruction quality, registration accuracy, and partial-volume effects. This sampling allows spatial contrast variations to be examined within NMC secondary particles with a median diameter of _D_ 50 = 4 _._ 7 _µ_ m. 

Voxel-wise calculation of the normalized Ni K-edge contrast requires spatial alignment of the volumes acquired at the three energies. Registration was performed using _elastix_ , with an initial rigid transformation followed by nonrigid B-spline registration. The aligned volumes were then combined to calculate the relative lithiation indicator defined below. Residual misalignment and interpolation may affect local contrast, particularly near particle boundaries, pores, and cracks. Because the three volumes were acquired sequentially, the resulting maps represent the electrode over a finite acquisition interval; registration corrects spatial mismatch but does not account for possible chemical evolution during this interval.

## **3 Results** 

## **3.1 Three-energy mapping of lithiation-sensitive contrast** 

The three-energy approach couples Ni K-edge sensitivity with three-dimensional imaging of electrode morphology. Reference XANES spectra of the pristine and charged electrodes set 8.30, 8.35, and 8.43 keV as the pre-edge, near-edge, and post-edge acquisition energies, respectively (Figure 1). The near-edge signal tracks the local nickel environment as lithium is extracted and reinserted[41]. 

5 

After reconstruction and inter-energy registration, the three volumes were combined into the normalized contrast _R_ defined in Equation 1. The resulting maps trace spatial variations in lithiation-sensitive contrast within and between secondary particles. Previous studies support Ni K-edge spectral features as trackers of electrochemical state;[42, 43] the fixed-energy contrast used here nonetheless remains a relative lithiation indicator, not a calibrated measurement of absolute lithium stoichiometry. 

The approach was applied to two composite electrodes: MX-A, with an active-material loading of 3 mg cm _[−]_[2] , measured in an AMPIX cell, and MX-B, with a loading of 15 mg cm _[−]_[2] , measured in TuCell after laser patterning. Lithiation heterogeneity could thus be examined under two loading and imaging conditions. The electrodes also differed in cell assembly, preparation, and discharge protocol, so their comparison does not isolate the effect of loading alone. 

Every map was acquired at open circuit after a 1 h relaxation period. What they reveal is the heterogeneity that survives current interruption. The following sections follow its evolution within the repeatedly imaged MX-A region and its distribution across the sampled regions of MX-B.

Repeated imaging of one MX-A electrode region allowed lithiation-sensitive contrast to be followed across successive discharge states. Discharge steps ran at an areal current density of 2 mA cm _[−]_[2] , and each acquisition followed a 1 h relaxation period at open circuit. To extend these observations beyond selected particles, secondary particles were individually segmented and labeled (Figure 4a). Their lithium-rich volume fractions increase overall from state I to state IV, across the sampled range of particle volumes (Figure 4b). The spread at each state reveals particle-to-particle variability but no systematic dependence on particle size.

Figure 4c shows their spatial distributions; Figure 4d summarizes their volume fractions. During discharge, the lithium-rich fraction grows as the lithiumpoor fraction shrinks. More broadly, reconstruction regularization, partial-volume effects, and inter-energy registration can all shape peripheral contrast. ## **4 Discussion** 

## **Lithiation heterogeneity across particle and electrode scales.** 

The reconstructed volumes show lithiation-sensitive contrast variations within secondary particles, between particles, and between electrode regions. In MX-A, the increase in the lithium-rich volume fraction during discharge is accompanied by nonuniform spatial distributions, including differences between particle interiors and peripheral regions. In MX-B, regions IIIa and IIIb exhibit different contrast distributions at the same nominal discharge state. These observations illustrate how an electrode-level electrochemical state can encompass a range of local lithiation states. Spatially resolved XANES measurements have previously revealed intraparticle state-of-charge heterogeneity in polycrystalline, nickel-rich layered oxides[31]. The present results extend this discussion to the spatial distributions observed in the investigated NMC532 composite electrodes. Their relevance lies in the coexistence of heterogeneity at several scales: an increase in the overall lithiation indicator does not imply that all particles, or all regions within a particle, evolve uniformly. 

Here, these distributions are inferred from relative Ni K-edge contrast rather than independently calibrated lithium concentrations. The regional differences observed in MX-B are relevant to the broader question of reaction uniformity in composite electrodes. Combined imaging and modeling of thick NMC811 cathodes has linked heterogeneous reaction distributions to the organization and evolution of ionic and electronic transport networks [45]. Our maps provide spatial information relevant to this problem, although they do not identify the transport process responsible for the observed differences. Moreover, MX-A and MX-B differ in cell configuration, sample preparation, and discharge conditions as well as loading. Their comparison therefore does not establish a loading-dependent increase in heterogeneity. 

**Core-shell-like distributions and nonuniform peripheral contrast.** 

The core-shell-like patterns observed in MX-A are consistent with differences in lithiation between the peripheral and interior regions of secondary particles. However, the peripheral contrast is not uniform around every particle. The observed distributions therefore suggest deviations from an idealized, radially symmetric description, rather than establishing a uniformly advancing lithiation front. Several factors could contribute to these patterns. Mistry _et al._ [46] showed through modeling that particle asphericity can generate nonuniform intercalation. Jiang _et al._ [44] demonstrated that particle detachment from the carbon–binder domain can alter the distribution of local electrical resistance. These studies provide possible interpretive frameworks for spatially heterogeneous lithiation. The present measurements, however, do not separate the effects of particle geometry, electronic connectivity, electrolyte accessibility, and intraparticle transport. 

Higher contrast near some internal pores also raises the possibility of lithium access through internal interfaces, consistent with previous reports of grain-boundary penetration.[47] Such spatial associations do not establish that these pores are electrolyte-connected or electrochemically active. Likewise, lower contrast near a particle boundary cannot be interpreted directly as an inactive surface. The maps record a local chemical state resulting from the preceding electrochemical history and subsequent redistribution, rather than the instantaneous interfacial reaction rate. 

## **Interpretation of heterogeneity after current interruption.** 

The measurement protocol is central to the interpretation of these distributions. Post-charge and discharge acquisitions were performed at zero applied current after a 1 h relaxation period. The maps therefore characterize states observed after relaxation; they do not directly capture the distributions present during galvanostatic cycling. Further redistribution may also occur during the sequential acquisition of the three tomographic datasets. 

Recent experiments and simulations on faceted nickel-rich NMC particles have highlighted 

10 

the contribution of interparticle exchange to lithium redistribution during rest.[48] Although the particle morphology and composition differ from those investigated here, this work emphasizes that zero external current does not necessarily imply an absence of local lithium redistribution. Accordingly, the heterogeneity observed after 1 h does not establish either complete equilibration or irreversible particle deactivation. 

The measured distributions should also be considered alongside the electrochemical response and sampling conditions. In MX-A, the pronounced polarization may include contributions from stack compression and interfacial contact in the AMPIX configuration, although these contributions were not isolated. In MX-B, changes in the sampled region limit direct comparisons between successive states. In particular, differences between pristine and discharged contrast distributions cannot be attributed exclusively to cycling. The comparison between regions IIIa and IIIb instead documents spatial variability at a common nominal discharge state. 

## **Implications and scope of three-energy chemical imaging.** 

These observations underline the value of describing lithiation through spatial distributions alongside electrode-averaged electrochemical quantities. Particle-averaged values can conceal internal variations, while electrode-averaged values can conceal differences between particles and regions. Three-dimensional imaging provides complementary information by locating these variations within the reconstructed morphology. 

For modeling, the observed patterns motivate consideration of departures from idealized spherical particles and uniform local boundary conditions. They do not, by themselves, invalidate homogenized electrode descriptions, whose suitability depends on the scale and observable of interest. Nor do they determine an electrochemically active surface fraction or identify a unique transport limitation. Their contribution is to document spatial features that such descriptions may need to account for when local lithiation distributions are the target observable. 

The three-energy acquisition provides this information with reduced spectral sampling, but its contrast remains a relative lithiation indicator. The lithium-poor, moderately lithiated, and lithium-rich classes are operational categories, not distinct phases or absolute stoichiometric intervals. The processed voxel size of around 200 nm is also a sampling interval rather than an independently established chemical resolution. Reconstruction, registration, partial-volume effects, and segmentation uncertainty warrant particular caution near particle boundaries, pores, and cracks. 

## **5 Conclusion** 

Three-energy XANES nanotomography enabled three-dimensional characterization of lithiation heterogeneity in NMC532 composite electrodes containing 96 wt% active material, with loadings of 3 and 15 mg cm _[−]_[2] and porosities of 30% and 28%, respectively. Acquisitions at 8.30, 8.35, and 8.43 keV combined particle morphology with Ni K-edge chemical sensitivity using three tomograms per measurement state. A processed voxel size of 210 nm allowed lithiation-sensitive contrast variations to be examined within secondary particles with a median diameter of 4.7 _µ_ m. 

In the lower-loading MX-A electrode, four successive states during interrupted discharge at 2 mA cm _[−]_[2] showed an increase in the lithium-rich volume fraction and a decrease in the lithiumpoor fraction. This overall progression was accompanied by spatially nonuniform lithiation: comparatively lithium-rich peripheral regions coexisted with less-lithiated particle interiors, while peripheral enrichment varied around individual particles. Particle-resolved analysis further revealed differences in lithium-rich volume fraction between particles at each examined state. Together, these results show that the evolution of an electrode-averaged lithiation indicator can conceal both internal particle gradients and variability within the particle population. 

The higher-loading MX-B electrode extended the measurements to 15 mg cm _[−]_[2] . Its contrast distributions changed consistently with lithium extraction during charge and reinsertion during discharge. At 50% depth of discharge, regions IIIa and IIIb displayed different distributions, with more extensive lithium-rich domains in region IIIa. This regional comparison demonstrates that a common nominal electrochemical state does not uniquely describe the local lithiation state throughout the sampled electrode. The different cell configurations, sample preparations, and discharge conditions nevertheless prevent attributing differences between MX-A and MX-B to electrode loading alone. 

The contribution of this study is therefore to connect lithiation-sensitive distributions across intraparticle, interparticle, and regional scales within composite electrodes. The maps were acquired after 1 h of open-circuit relaxation and describe the states observed after this interval, rather than instantaneous reaction distributions under current. Although the normalized contrast 

11 

is a relative indicator rather than an independently calibrated absolute lithium concentration, it provides spatial information complementary to the global electrochemical response and reveals heterogeneity that is not captured by a single mean lithiation value. 

A promising extension is correlative XANES and X-ray phase-contrast nanotomography of the same electrode volume. With suitable spectral calibration, XANES could provide estimates of local lithium content, while phase contrast could improve visualization of the weakly absorbing carbon–binder domain (CBD), particle–CBD contacts, and surrounding pores. Registering these complementary datasets would enable direct spatial comparisons between lithiation patterns and the local electrode architecture. Such measurements could help determine whether the observed peripheral and regional heterogeneities are associated with differences in particle connectivity or pore accessibility, and how these associations evolve during cycling and relaxation.
