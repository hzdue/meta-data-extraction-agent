Resonant inelastic x-ray scattering (RIXS) has become a prominent technique to study quasiparticle excitations. With advances in polarization analysis capabilities at different facilities, RIXS offers exceptional potential for investigating symmetry-broken quasiparticles like chiral phonons and magnons. At optical wavelengths birefringence can severely affect polarization states in lowsymmetry systems. Here we show its importance for soft x-ray resonances. Given the growing interest in Circular Dichroism (CD) in RIXS, it is important to evaluate how birefringence may affect the RIXS spectra of anisotropic systems. We investigate CuO, a well-known anisotropic material, using Cu _L_ 3-edge RIXS and detect significant CD in both magnetic and orbital excitations in the collinear antiferromagnetic phase. We demonstrate that the CD can be modeled by a proper treatment of RIXS scattering amplitudes derived from single-ion calculations with birefringence. Recognizing these effects is crucial for unambiguous identification of subtle dichroic effects induced by symmetry-broken quasiparticles. Furthermore, the combined sensitivity of RIXS and birefringence to local symmetry presents an opportunity to study microscopic changes driven by external perturbations. 

Symmetry constraints of light-matter interactions are fundamental to optical spectroscopic methods. Most materials belong to non-cubic crystal systems and thus exhibit anisotropic responses to light, which is useful for understanding the symmetry based origins of physical properties. At optical wavelengths dichroism (polarization dependent absorption) and birefringence (polarization dependent phase retardation) are two of such commonly utilized anisotropic responses to light [1]. 

In the x-ray regime, the anisotropic responses are usually negligible since the refractive index is very close to unity, the value for vacuum. Near the resonant transitions from core shells to valence states in atoms, however, the polarization states of x-rays become highly sensitive to local symmetry. Dichroism spectroscopy with linearly polarized X-ray can be used to investigate orientational order of anisotropic motifs, e.g., X-ray Natural Linear Dichroism, to study element specific orbital orientation along corresponding symmetry axes [2] and X-ray Magnetic Linear Dichroism to investigate easy spin axis anisotropy [3]. Dichroism spectroscopy with circularly polarized x-rays like X-ray Magnetic Circular Dichroism and X-ray Natural Circular Dichroism can be used to study element-specific magnetic and charge density anisotropy by detecting time-reversal or space-inversion symmetry-breaking, respectively [4, 5]. While these techniques are limited to ground state anisotropies, the development of RIXS has extended the possibilities to study symmetry-broken quasiparticles like phonons and magnons [11, 12]. For instance, long-sought chiral phonons were recently observed in space-inversion symmetry-broken _α_ -quartz using CD in RIXS [13]. Circular Dichroic-RIXS (CD-RIXS) has been proposed to probe the local orbital angular momentum and associated Berry curvature in inversion symmetry-broken transition metal dichalcogenides [14], and also to identify different orbital ordered states based on the symmetry of occupied orbitals [15]. In principle, the presence of the recently proposed chiral magnons in time-reversal symmetry broken altermagnets [16] can also be tested using CD-RIXS. 

The wavelength dependent complex linear absorption coefficient of a material can be written as _µ_ = _µ[′]_ + _iµ[′′]_ . The polarization dependence in _µ[′]_ ( _µ[′′]_ ) represents dichroism (birefringence) [17–19], and can be related to the ˜ complex refractive index _n_ = 1 _−_ [ _λ/_ 4 _π_ ] _µ[′′]_ + _i_ [ _λ/_ 4 _π_ ] _µ[′]_ in this notation. Typically, _Re_ (˜ _n_ ) _≈_ 1 at these wavelengths, and therefore birefringence effects are regularly neglected in X-ray studies. However, near X-ray resonances, anomalous dispersion may lead to significant enhancement in birefringence [17, 19–25]. Birefringence has been utilized to develop molecular anisotropy imaging techniques [25], proposed for making non-diffractive x-ray phase retarders [20] and actively searched as a signature of translational symmetry breaking of vacuum in presence of strong electromagnetic fields from the propositions of non-linear quantum electrodynamics [26].

While x-ray birefringence can be utilized for its symmetry dependence, it may also obscure effects from important microscopic symmetry-breaking phenomena, leading to conflicting interpretations. A notable example is from the studies of CuO. Initially studied as a model material to understand the physical properties of cuprate superconductors from the viewpoint of Cu-O electronic structure, it was found to exhibit multiferroicity due to space-inversion symmetry breaking induced by spinspiral ordering within the temperature range of 213-230 K [27, 28]. Many of its properties are easily controllable: the multiferroic phase can be extended to room temperature under pressure [29], magnetic order can be controlled optically [30], and chiral magnetic domains and optical activity can be tuned by applying an electric field [31, 32]. Crystallographically, CuO has been described in both centrosymmetric ( _C_ 2/ _c_ ) and noncentrosymmetric ( _Cc_ ) monoclinic space groups [33]. Below 213 K, CuO adopts a collinear antiferromagnetic ordered (AF1) phase. In this phase, the local space and time-inversion symmetries can be broken, making it a candidate for possessing atomic magnetoelectric multipoles as tested recently with neutron scattering [34]. A strong CD was observed in REXS measurements at the antiferromagnetic ordering vector **q** AFM (0.5, 0, -0.5) for the globally time-reversal symmetry invariant AF1 phase, even though CD should be zero in the electric-dipole approximation [35, 36]. This observation was initially attributed to electric-dipole magnetic-dipole scattering from the symmetry allowed toroidal moments, one of the atomic magnetoelectric multipoles [35]. However, the claim was later challenged due to potential contributions from birefringence [17, 18, 21], leaving the origin of CD in REXS unresolved. 

While treatment of x-ray birefringence has been reported for X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy (XAS), x-ray reflectivity and REXS [17, 19–25], its effects have never been observed in or considered for inelastic x-ray spectroscopy. Given the growing interest in CD-RIXS, it is crucial to determine whether and how birefringence affects the polarization dependence of RIXS spectra in anisotropic systems. In this work, we present the incident polarization dependent RIXS spectra at the Cu _L_ 3-edge of CuO. We observe significant CD for both magnetic and orbital excitations in the AF1 phase. We demonstrate that the observed CD in RIXS can be modeled by considering x-ray birefringence resulting in excitation dependent CD, which arises from the anisotropyinduced change in x-ray polarization when x-rays propagate through the material. Consequently, careful consideration must be given to the presence of birefringence and its impact on the quantitative estimation of polarization dependent RIXS spectra of low-symmetry systems.

Similar to these works, we have approximated the nearly square CuO4 plaquettes of the highly distorted Cu-O octahedra to have _D_ 4 _h_ symmetry to simulate the RIXS spectra from CuO using the single-ion multiplet code EDRIXS [43] (see SM [39]). The magnetic excitations in CuO comprise both well-defined three-dimensional magnons and one-dimensional spinon continuum [27]. In our calculations, the magnetic excitations are the local spin-flip amplitudes that scale the intrinsic dynamic spin susceptibility in the RIXS process [44]. 

In Fig. 1(c), RIXS spectra obtained with different incident polarizations at **q** = (0.45, 0, -0.45) with incident energy _E_ in = 928 eV, _T_ = 20 K and _ϕ_ = 60 _[◦]_ are shown. Strong linear dichroism typical of cuprates, is experimentally observed for the magnetic (0.1 eV) and the orbital (1.4-2.4 eV) excitations [see Fig. 1(c)], which is also replicated in the simulated RIXS spectra shown in Fig. 1(d). RIXS CD calculated as _I_ diff _/I_[¯] sum, where _I_ diff = _I_ CL _−I_ CR and _I_[¯] sum is the mean of ( _I_ CL + _I_ CR) over the energy loss ( _ω_ ) range is plotted in Fig. 1(e). Surprisingly, both the magnetic and the orbital excitations exhibit significant circular contrast. However, the simulated RIXS spectra for CL and CR polarizations are identical [see Fig. 1(d, f)], as expected from electric-dipole scattering in the AF1 phase. For REXS at **q** AFM contributions from higherorder scattering due to toroidal moments in CuO was estimated to be four orders of magnitude smaller than that of the electric-dipole contributions [21, 45], though absolute estimations of higher-order transitions are not expected to be accurate. Given that CuO is a monoclinic (biaxial) system, we therefore investigate whether the observed RIXS CD is due to birefringence. In RIXS simulations, during x-ray propagation through a material, the only effect considered in previous works is the effect of absorption while the polarization state is kept intact. To model the birefringence or the depth dependent polarization change, we use the transfer matrix formalism developed by Lovesey and Collins for x-rays based on Jones calculus [19, 46, 47], and adapt the matrices to calculate RIXS single-ion atomic scattering factors. Fig. 2(d) shows the RIXS spectra calculated using Eq. 3 for CL and CR polarizations and the same experimental conditions as in Fig. 1(c). The resulting RIXS CD is plotted in Fig. 2(e). Considering the polarization state change induced by birefringence, CD is now observed for both the magnetic and the orbital excitations in excellent agreement with the experiment. To demonstrate the validity of our approach, we present in Fig. 3(a), the experimentally obtained RIXS CD at different _ϕ_ s for **q** = (0.45, 0, -0.45) and _E_ in = 928 eV. The markers in Fig. 3(b) and (c) show the experimental _ϕ_ dependence of RIXS CD asymmetry _≡ I_[¯] diff _/I_[¯] sum for the magnetic and the orbital excitations, respectively. The solid curves in the panels show the RIXS CD calculated using Eq. 3. Experimentally, the CD signal for both types of excitations goes from negative to positive and then negative again in the probed _ϕ_ range. The calculated RIXS CD follows the experimental _ϕ_ dependence excellently both in sign as well as in magnitude as shown by the line profiles in (a) panels and the summed values in panels (b) and (c). 

Further confirmation of the birefringence origin of the RIXS CD is obtained from its _E_ in dependence presented in Fig. 4(a) for **q** = (0.45, 0, -0.45) at _ϕ_ = 60 _[◦]_ . The RIXS CD changes sign as _E_ in is scanned across the Cu _L_ 3-edge. For a _d_[9] system the absorption peak comprises of a resonant excitation to a single multiplet state. It is therefore unlikely for any other symmetry-breaking phenomena (like the development of toroidal moments) to induce such a sign change due to interference among different multiplet states. Instead, _µ[′′]_ which is related by the Kramers-Kronig transformation to _µ[′]_ , undergoes a sign change across the absorption edge [see inset to Fig. 1(d)]. At the resonance, where _µ[′′]_ is zero, the birefringence contribution is zero and so is the RIXS CD. The experimentally observed sign change across the Cu L3-resonance therefore appears in our calculations when birefringence is included. Note that the RIXS CD is also observed in the paramagnetic phase at _T_ = 240 _K_ [see SM [39]], also supporting the birefringence interpretation. 

Using the framework developed for birefringence in RIXS with single-ion calculations we were also able to simulate the REXS CD of CuO at **q** AFM [35, 36] like earlier theoretical works [17, 18, 21] (see SM [39]). However, it should be noted that away from the XAS peak at _E_ in = 929 eV the calculated RIXS CD grows rapidly compared to the experiment (see SM [39]). This is due to the strongly decaying single-ion absorption that results in a rapid enhancement of the penetration depth (1/ _µ_ 0 in Eq. 3). In the real material contribution from oxygen and continuum states represented by a step function over the edge, lead to a slower variation of _µ_ 0 away from the resonance where the polarization change occurs [48]. When a step function representing the transitions to these states is considered so that the penetra- 

tion depth is 1 _/_ ( _µ_ 0 + step), we observe a significant improvement in the energy dependent RIXS CD from Fig. 4 (b) and (c) (see SM for the details [39]). This suggests that although our calculations do not contain any free scaling parameter, to exactly determine the magnitude of birefringence-induced RIXS CD, precise knowledge of the energy-dependent penetration depth is crucial. This is unfortunately difficult to obtain experimentally for insulating materials like CuO (electron yield is preferable over fluorescence yield to minimize self-absorption and birefringence effects), or one needs inputs from firstprinciples electronic structure theories [21]. Therefore, our calculations do not invalidate a finite presence of other symmetry-breaking contribution to the observed RIXS or REXS CD [34, 35]. In addition, experimentally sampling multiple domains can lead to scaling of the CD magnitude [36, 39], further increasing the uncertainty in estimating the contribution from the higher-order scatterings. 

To conclude, we demonstrate experimentally the presence of significant CD in the RIXS spectra of CuO at the Cu _L_ 3-edge in its collinear antiferromagnetic phase. Our simulations show that the CD can be well-described by birefringence from modelling the RIXS scattering amplitudes derived from single-ion models, as detailed in Eq. 3. This approach offers a framework for detecting or predicting the impact of birefringence in RIXS experiments. This finding emphasizes the need to account for birefringence when performing quantitative polarization analysis of RIXS spectra (including linear dichroism) from any low-symmetry system. Experimental conditions should therefore be chosen carefully to mitigate the birefringence effects while searching for other subtle symmetry-breaking phenomena associated with the quasiparticle excitations. Propagating x-rays along the highest symmetry direction can reduce the effect of birefringence for uniaxial systems. While limiting the study to the peak of _L_ 3-edge XAS can help eliminate it for _d_[9] systems, and it is important to note that for lower electronic configurations, birefringence may exhibit a more complex energy dependence due to interference among different multiplet states [49, 50], requiring more careful considerations. Nonetheless, birefringence in RIXS could serve as a valuable tool in condensed matter physics, allowing the study of quasiparticle dynamics and their response to subtle changes in local symmetry induced by variations in temperature, pressure, or light pulses.

**I. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS** 

A polished single crystal of CuO grown using floating-zone technique was used for the experiment [1]. All measurements were done at I21 beamline, Diamond Light Source, UK [2]. The sample was cooled down to 20 K for measurements in the AF1 phase. The pressure inside the sample vessel was maintained around 5 _×_ 10 _[−]_[10] mbar. The x-ray absorption (XAS) was collected as fluorescence yield (FY) using a photodiode. High energy-resolution (∆ _E_ = 0 _._ 035 eV) RIXS spectra were collected at Cu _L_ 3-edge. The zero-energy transfer position and energy resolution were determined from subsequent measurements of elastic peaks from an adjacent carbon tape. 

The vertical (horizontal) x-ray spot size on the sample was around 5 _µ_ m (35 _µ_ m) at normal incidence. The magnetic domain dimensions in CuO crystals have been seen to greater than 100 _µ_ m [3], and thus it is likely that we have sampled majorly a single domain throughout the experiment. 

The low-energy RIXS features were fit using a Gaussian line-shape for the elastic peak, and Voigt line-shapes for the magnetic peaks (magnon and spinon contributions [4]) [see Fig. S1]. Using the fitted elastic peak position we could align the CL and CR RIXS spectra to an accuracy of about 0.005 eV. The fitted elastic peak was subtracted from the RIXS spectra when calculating RIXS CD that have been presented in the manuscript. Note that no self-absorption correction was applied to the experimental data. 

In the manuscript we have presented the RIXS CD spectra for the azimuthal and energy dependence. The experimental and calculated RIXS spectra corresponding to the azimuthal dependence is shown in Fig. S2 and the energy dependence is shown in Fig. S3. 

Additional RIXS data were collected at 240 K in the paramagnetic phase at different azimuthal ( _ϕ_ ) angles which also show the presence of RIXS CD as shown in Fig. S4. Note that the thermal expansion of the sample manipulator between 20 K and 240 K can lead to measurement at a slightly different spot, even though measures were taken to correct for the drift in the sample position. 

## **II. THEORY DETAILS** 

As mentioned in the manuscript we have approximated the Cu site with a _D_ 4 _h_ symmetry for the calculations. Similar higher symmetries as compared to _C_ 1 in the real material were found to satisfactorily explain earlier nonresonant inelastic x-ray scattering results on CuO [5]. The orbital ( _dd_ ) excitations in this case correspond to spin-flip and non-spin-flip excitations to the _dxy_ , _dyz_ , _dxz_ and the _d_ 3 _z_ 2 _−r_ 2 orbitals from the _dx_ 2 _−y_ 2 orbital. In the past, energies of around 1.8, 2.1, and 2.2 eV have been attributed to excitations to the _dxy_ , _dyz_ and _dxz_ , and _d_ 3 _z_ 2 _−r_ 2 orbitals respectively, from the investigation of the orbital states in CuO using RIXS, non-resonant inelastic x-ray scattering and quantum chemistry calculations [5–7]. We have considered four plaquettes as shown in Fig. 1(a) of the manuscript to simulate the XAS and RIXS spectra from CuO using the single-ion multiplet code EDRIXS [8]. A plaquette dependent directional exchange field was added to the calculations to represent the local spin-flip RIXS amplitude that scales the polarization or _E_ in independent dynamic spin susceptibility to give the experimental RIXS spin-wave intensity [9].
